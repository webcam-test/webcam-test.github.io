How to Use Your Phone as a Webcam on Windows and Mac

This guide shows you how to use phone as webcam — covering built-in options, third-party apps, and a wired vs wireless setup comparison. Nothing you do in the test my webcam ever leaves your device — the whole test runs locally in your browser tab.

Which Method for Your Devices

Find your phone + computer combination below

iPhone + MacContinuity Camera — built into macOS 13+ and iOS 16+, no app needed. Your iPhone appears automatically as a camera option in any app.
Android + Windows or MacA third-party app (Camo, DroidCam, Iriun and similar) installed on both the phone and computer, usually connecting over USB or Wi-Fi.
iPhone + WindowsA third-party app is required since there's no built-in bridge — the same category of app as the Android + Windows row, with an iOS companion app.
Any phone, wired for lowest latencyPrefer a USB connection over Wi-Fi where the app supports it — noticeably lower latency and no dependence on network conditions.

Why Bother

Most laptop webcams are a low-priority afterthought in a laptop's design, while phone cameras get significant engineering investment every generation — for most people, even a phone that's a few years old outperforms their laptop's built-in camera.

The camera in your pocket almost certainly beats the one built into your laptop lid, so when you use your phone as a webcam you get a sharper, brighter picture without buying anything. Your smartphone can stand in for a USB webcam on iPhone and Android alike — below you'll find every working route, from built-in options to apps and a browser trick, plus the settings that keep it steady through a long call.

Why a Phone Camera Beats a Laptop Webcam on Video Quality

Notebook cameras are squeezed into a thin bezel; a phone gets a larger sensor, better optics and serious image processing. That gives you: Nothing you do in the webcam filters ever leaves your device — the whole test runs locally in your browser tab.

  • Better low-light performance in a dim room or on a winter afternoon
  • Sharper detail and cleaner image quality at 1080p
  • Reliable autofocus when you lean in
  • More accurate white balance and natural skin tones

For the best result, point the rear camera at yourself — it is almost always the stronger sensor. You give up seeing yourself on the handset, but everyone else on the call notices the difference.

What You Need to Use Your Phone as a Webcam

A reliable phone-as-webcam setup takes six things, most of which you already own: Nothing you do in the test your phone's front camera ever leaves your device — the whole test runs locally in your browser tab.

  • A supported handset — any recent iPhone on current iOS, or a handset running Android 10 or newer.
  • A webcam app or built-in option (each is covered below).
  • A tripod, clamp mount or phone stand — leaning the handset on a mug costs you stability the moment you bump the desk.
  • A charging cable, because continuous capture empties a charge quickly.
  • Lighting from the front: a window facing you or a lamp behind the screen.
  • A separate microphone, such as a lapel mic, if audio matters — phone mics pick up keyboard clatter.

Check your connection too: Zoom lists roughly 3.8 Mbps of upload speed for 1080p streaming, and every other device on the network eats into that bandwidth.

iPhone as a Webcam on Mac and Windows

Apple builds a route into its own computers; Windows needs a third-party app. Check the webcam not working troubleshooting guide if you want the background explanation before running a live test elsewhere on this site.

Continuity Camera on a Mac

Apple's Continuity Camera works with an iPhone XR or later on iOS 16 and macOS Ventura or later, with nothing to download:

  1. Sign both devices into the same Apple ID and turn on Wi-Fi and Bluetooth.
  2. Mount the handset horizontally and locked, rear lens facing you.
  3. Open your call app's camera list and pick the phone — it shows up automatically.

Camo or EpocCam with Companion Software on Windows

Install Camo or EpocCam on the phone and the matching companion software, which installs a driver, on the PC.

  • Connect with a USB cable for the steadiest picture and least lag, or join the same Wi-Fi network for a wireless link.
  • In your call app, choose the app's device as the video source.

Android as a Webcam: Three Ways to Connect

An Android phone as a webcam has more routes: Microsoft's phone pairing, the native UVC mode in newer releases, and cross-platform apps.

Link to Windows and Phone Link on Windows 11

  1. Install the Link to Windows app on the phone and sign in.
  2. On the PC, open Settings > Bluetooth & devices > Mobile devices, and choose Add device if the phone isn't listed yet.
  3. Select your phone and switch on Use as connected camera.

Windows then treats your mobile device as a webcam for every app on the computer.

USB Preferences: Android 14's Built-In UVC Mode

Phones on Android 14 (QPR1) or later can use a device as a webcam with no app at all. The phone presents itself as a standard UVC device — the same class ordinary plug-in cameras use — so Windows, macOS, Linux and ChromeOS accept it with their own UVC driver.

  1. Plug the phone in and tap the connection notification.
  2. Under USB preferences, choose the webcam option.
  3. Use the on-phone preview to pick the front or back lens, set the zoom level and tap to focus.

The webcam feed leaves the phone as MJPEG or uncompressed YUYV. Over USB 2.0 (480 Mbps), uncompressed tops out at 720p at 30 fps, while MJPEG's compression leaves room for full HD and beyond at the same frame rate — so choose the highest resolution your call app offers. Developers can switch modes with adb shell svc usb setFunctions uvc.

DroidCam and Iriun Client Software

On older handsets, or to go wireless, an app does the job on any desktop:

  • DroidCam: install it on the phone and its client software on the computer, then connect by cable or over the local network.
  • Iriun: finds the phone on your network automatically and appears as an Android webcam device in call apps.

Wireless is fine for a test; a cable gives fewer dropped connections in a long session, and these apps work on phones that predate UVC mode.

Browser Source and Stream URL: A Virtual Camera in OBS

No install at all? A browser-based streamer turns any phone into a scene source: open the service's page on the phone, allow access, and copy its stream URL.

  1. In the sources panel, add a new Browser source and paste the link.
  2. Hold the phone in landscape mode so the image fills a 16:9 canvas.
  3. Click Start Virtual Camera so Google Meet, Zoom or any call app sees the composed scene.

Pick this route when you can't install desktop software: the phone needs only a browser and, drawing no picture of its own while streaming, runs cool with low latency. It suits a livestream, e-commerce product clips or live streaming with overlays.

Choosing Your Phone Webcam in Video Settings and the Camera Dropdown

Whichever route you pick, the last step happens inside your streaming app or video conferencing tool, and each one hides the camera input in a slightly different place:

  • Zoom: preferences, then the video tab, then pick the phone from the list.
  • Discord: user settings, the voice and video page, then the phone as your device.
  • Google Meet: the three-dot menu, then the camera field.

Before meetings that matter, run a short test meeting: a two-minute rehearsal shows whether the right source, mic and framing are selected before a real video call, and a quick recording lets you hear what others hear.

Camera Angle, Framing and Lighting for a Mobile Device

Once connected, where you put the phone matters more than the app. For coaching or a sales call, raise the phone on its tripod until the rear lens — which sits off-centre on most handsets — lines up with your eyes. For online classes that involve movement, move the phone back for a full-body shot and tape a mark on the floor so you stay inside it.

  • Keep your eyes near the top third of the frame.
  • Clamp the phone sideways; a portrait image gets pillar-boxed in most call apps.
  • Avoid backlight from a window behind you — turn to face it instead.
  • Lock exposure in the phone's camera app so brightness doesn't pulse as you move.

Fixing Common Problems with Your Phone's Camera

Most glitches come down to access prompts, power or audio routing.

Camera Permissions and Screen Rotation

If the call shows a black placeholder, the phone is waiting for permission to share its lens. Look for a prompt on the phone or a new window on the PC taskbar; if nothing appears, open the phone's app settings and turn camera permissions on for the companion app. If the picture won't switch between landscape and portrait, check that the rotation lock is off.

Battery, Thermal Limits and Dropped Frames

  • Keep the phone plugged in for anything longer than a short call.
  • Take off the case and keep it out of the sun: the sensor's power consumption builds heat, and Android lowers quality once thermal limits are hit.
  • Use Do Not Disturb or airplane mode so notifications don't interrupt the stream.
  • On Apple computers, Android's UVC mode can fail with USB 3.0 cables because of a bug in Apple's UVC driver — use a USB 2.0 cable instead.
  • If latency creeps up, move from wireless to the cable.

Echo, Focus Hunting and Background Blur

Echo means the phone's mic and the desktop mic are both live: mute one in the companion app. If the phone's lens keeps hunting, add light or tap the subject in the on-phone preview, and if a virtual background hides part of you, turn it off.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you use a smartphone as a webcam?
Yes — modern smartphones have cameras that far exceed most built-in laptop webcams. Using a free or low-cost app, you can connect your iPhone or Android phone to your PC or Mac via USB or Wi-Fi and use it as a high-quality webcam for video calls, streaming, or recording.
What are the requirements for using my phone as a webcam?
You need a compatible phone (iPhone or Android), the appropriate app installed on both your phone and computer, and either a USB cable or a shared Wi-Fi network. For iPhone on Mac (macOS Ventura+), Continuity Camera is built in and requires no extra app. Samsung Galaxy users on One UI 6.1+ can use the native Camera Sharing feature.
How do I use my iPhone as a webcam on a Mac?
If your Mac runs macOS Ventura or later and your iPhone runs iOS 16+, Apple's Continuity Camera feature works automatically over Wi-Fi — no app needed. Just unlock your iPhone near your Mac and it will appear as a camera option in FaceTime, Zoom, and other apps. For older systems, apps like Camo or EpocCam work well.
How do I use an Android phone as a webcam on Windows?
For Windows 11 with a recent Android phone, the Phone Link / Microsoft Phone app integration is the easiest route. Third-party apps like DroidCam and EpocCam also work on both Windows 10 and 11. Samsung Galaxy users (One UI 6.1+) can use the built-in Camera Sharing feature wirelessly with compatible Galaxy tablets or Galaxy Book laptops.
Can I use my phone as a webcam via USB?
Yes, connecting via USB is actually recommended for the lowest latency and most stable stream. Apps like DroidCam (Android) and EpocCam (iPhone) support USB connections. On Android, you'll need to enable USB Debugging in Developer Options. USB is ideal for live streaming or any use case where a dropped Wi-Fi signal would be disruptive.
What is the best app for using my phone as a webcam for Zoom?
For iPhone on Mac, Apple's built-in Continuity Camera is the best option — no setup needed. For Windows users or older Macs, Camo (iPhone) and DroidCam (Android) are highly rated. EpocCam is also popular and integrates directly as a virtual camera that Zoom, Teams, and Google Meet all detect automatically.
Why is my phone screen not rotating when I flip it?
Most webcam apps lock the orientation to prevent accidental rotation mid-call. Check if auto-rotate is enabled in your phone's quick settings panel. Some apps like Camo have an explicit orientation lock option in their settings that you may need to toggle off.
My video background is blocking my image — what should I do?
If a virtual background is obscuring you, check the background settings within your video call app (Zoom, Teams, etc.) and disable or change the background effect. The issue is with the call app's background processing, not the phone camera itself. Ensure adequate lighting so the app can properly detect your outline.
