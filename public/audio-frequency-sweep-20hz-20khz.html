Understanding the Frequency Sweep Test

A sweep is defined by three numbers: where it starts, where it ends, and how long it takes to get there. Set a start frequency of 20 Hz and an end frequency of 20,000 Hz over 20 seconds, and the generator produces one continuous tone that rises smoothly across the full audible range — no gaps, no jumps, just a steady climb from bass to treble. Different names show up for this same utility: a frequency sweep generator, a sweep generator tool, a plain tone generator, a frequency generator, or simply a frequency sweep tool. Start Frequency, End Frequency, and Sweep Duration

Every sweep generator needs a start point, an end point, and a duration. Most default to the full audio bandwidth — roughly 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz — because that matches audible frequencies human ears can register (some tools label the second value the stop frequency instead). A bass-only sweep might run 20–200 Hz over 10 seconds; a tweeter check might run 2,000–20,000 Hz over 5 seconds. Making the sweep shorter doesn't skip frequencies, it just moves through them faster, which matters if you're recording the output for later analysis rather than listening by ear.

Choosing a Waveform: Sine Wave, Square, Sawtooth, or Triangle

The waveform shapes the tone's harmonic content, and most generators default to the purest option because it carries no extra harmonics — a single frequency at every instant, which is exactly what a clean measurement needs.

  • Sine — a single pure frequency with no added harmonics; the standard choice for measurement
  • Square wave — rich in odd harmonics, useful for stress-testing amplifiers and drivers
  • Sawtooth — contains both odd and even harmonics, closer to some real instrument timbres
  • Triangle — softer harmonic content than a square wave, gentler on tweeters during testing

Sine Sweep Types: Logarithmic, Linear, and Stepped

Not every sweep moves through frequency the same way. The scaling you pick determines whether bass, midrange, and treble each get equal scrutiny, or whether one end of the range dominates the sweep. Log Sweep vs. Linear Sweep Timing

A log sweep spends the same amount of time across every doubling of frequency — the climb from 20 Hz to 40 Hz takes exactly as long as the climb from 10,000 Hz to 20,000 Hz. A linear sweep instead spends equal time per Hz, which sounds like a small distinction until you see it plotted out.

This scaling gives bass, midrange, and treble equal time, which is why it's the default for room and system measurement. Linear scaling still has its place for spotting evenly spaced harmonic artifacts, since equal Hz spacing keeps those relationships visually consistent.

The relationship between time and instantaneous frequency for each type can be written directly. For a logarithmic sweep from \(f_0\) to \(f_1\) over duration \(T\):

$$ f(t) = f_0 \times \left(\frac{f_1}{f_0}\right)^{t/T} $$

For a linear sweep over the same range and duration:

$$ f(t) = f_0 + (f_1 - f_0) \times \frac{t}{T} $$

Stepped Sweep and Glide Sweep Measurement

A glide sweep (sometimes called a chirp) is continuous — frequency changes smoothly with no pause, which keeps signal length short without losing resolution. A stepped sweep instead holds each frequency steady, waits out a settling time before the reading is trusted, then jumps to the next step; it takes longer overall but is the standard for measuring how linearity holds at a fixed frequency while amplitude is varied.

  • Amplitude weighting — defines how the input level itself changes across the sweep, used for equalizing an amplifier or loudspeaker to a flat output
  • Time sweep — plots a measured value against elapsed time rather than frequency, useful for watching how a device's response drifts over a long session
  • Table sweep — a rarer form that steps through a predefined table of frequency-and-amplitude pairs instead of a continuous range

Reading Your Sweep Tone's Frequency Response

The value of a sweep isn't the tone itself — it's the response curve it produces once you plot output level against frequency. A flat line means uniform output at every point; anywhere the curve strays from flat marks a frequency worth investigating.

Spotting Dips, Peaks, and Resonant Frequency Issues

A narrow dip usually points to a room mode or a phase-cancellation null between a driver and a nearby wall. A sharp peak is more often a resonant frequency — a point where a driver, cabinet, or room dimension naturally wants to vibrate, amplifying whatever energy arrives at that frequency. Watching this on a live spectrum analyzer while the sweep plays, with peak-hold enabled, lets you catch a narrow spike that a slower ear-only pass would miss entirely — the full audio spectrum updates in real time as the tone climbs.

Real-World Ways to Use a Sweep Test Tone

Beyond a lab bench, a sweep answers practical questions: does this unit actually reach 30 Hz, is this the frequency my ringing matches, is this pair of headphones missing anything in the midrange.

Subwoofer Test and Bass Test for Low Bass Output

A subwoofer test narrows the sweep to roughly 20–200 Hz and listens for where output actually rolls off versus where the spec sheet claims it does. A bass test run this way also exposes port noise, cabinet buzz, or a driver reaching its excursion limit — problems that a single low note might not trigger but a full sweep through every bass frequency reliably will.

Tinnitus Frequency Matching and Hearing Checks

If a ring or hiss won't go away, a slow sweep through the range where it sits can help you match its exact pitch — check one octave above and below whatever frequency seems closest, since tones an octave apart are easy to confuse. Your sensitivity to different frequencies isn't flat either — a fact mapped by equal loudness contours — and a broader sweep across the full range makes that roll-off audible rather than theoretical. A step beyond casual listening is formal audiometry, where a trained tester controls level and frequency far more precisely than a browser tab can, useful once tinnitus matching needs a real diagnosis behind it.

Testing Audio Equipment, Playback Devices, and the Device Under Test

For audio equipment that has no direct input — a phone or a tablet, for instance — the sweep has to be pre-loaded onto the playback devices themselves as a file rather than fed live, since there's no closed signal path back to an analyzer. Whatever gear sits on the receiving end is the device under test, and everything the sweep measures is really a description of how that one device colors what passes through it.

Checking a Used Subwoofer Before Keeping It

A 12-inch sealed subwoofer shows up secondhand for $140, and the seller's only claim is "still hits hard." The cabinet is a Klipsch R-120SW, a model with a published low-frequency extension down to 29 Hz at -3 dB, so that number becomes the thing to verify before deciding whether the price was fair.

The sub gets placed in the same corner the old one occupied, a phone runs a room measurement app fed by a calibrated USB microphone, and the sweep generator is set to a narrow band: start frequency 18 Hz, end frequency 200 Hz, 15-second duration, logarithmic scaling, sine waveform. Eighteen Hz rather than 20 leaves a small margin below the spec sheet's number, in case the room itself is adding or masking anything right at the claimed cutoff.

Pressing play sends the tone climbing slowly through the room while the app logs output level every tenth of a second. The resulting curve sits flat and even from 200 Hz down to about 34 Hz, then begins rolling off — gently at first, then steeply — crossing the -3 dB line relative to the 60 Hz reference level at 31.6 Hz, not the 29 Hz on the spec sheet. There's also a narrow 6 dB dip sitting right at 41 Hz, which lines up with a known room mode for a 3.8-meter-long living room, not a flaw in the driver itself.

A 31.6 Hz measured extension against a 29 Hz claim is close enough to call the unit healthy — driver foam surrounds degrade with age and typically cost 2–4 Hz of low-end reach long before they cost 10 or more, and the 41 Hz dip is confirmed as a room artifact by re-running the same sweep with the subwoofer shifted 60 cm off the wall, which moves the dip's exact frequency instead of eliminating it. That combination — a small, explainable shortfall at the bottom end and a dip that's clearly the room's doing — is enough to keep the subwoofer and spend the leftover budget on a second unit for even bass distribution, rather than return it as defective.

Pink Noise, White Noise, and Other Sweep Test Tones

A sweep isn't the only test signal worth knowing. A few related tones show up constantly alongside it, and each isolates a different kind of problem.

Pink Noise and White Noise for Sound Testing

Unlike a sweep, which isolates one frequency at a time, white noise and pink noise play the entire spectrum at once: one spreads energy equally per Hz, the other spreads it equally per doubling of frequency — the same logic a log sweep applies one step at a time, covered in more depth under the colors of noise. Either gives a fast, rough read on overall balance, where a sweep instead isolates a single problem with much more energy behind it.

Harmonic Distortion and Intermodulation Distortion Sound Tests

A sweep also flags mechanical trouble indirectly — a driver that buzzes or rattles at one specific frequency during the climb is showing stress the flat sections don't reveal. For a direct reading, dedicated tones exist: one checks for unwanted overtones added at a single frequency, and another plays two tones together to check whether they interact and generate frequencies that shouldn't exist at all.

Curious Sound Tests: the Shepard Tone Illusion and Brown Note

Not everything in a test-tone library is diagnostic. The Shepard tone illusion is a stack of octaves crossfaded to sound like it climbs forever without ever actually rising, and the so-called brown note is internet folklore about an infrasonic frequency claimed to cause physical discomfort — neither has anything to do with equipment measurement, but both regularly turn up in the same libraries as sweeps and noise, which is worth knowing before you go looking for a high definition audio file of one.

Test Tone Safety and Sweep Signal Accuracy

This kind of test is only as useful as it is safe to run, and getting a clean reading out of one takes a bit more care than pressing play.

Volume Safety When Playing Test Tones

Set your signal level using a comfortable 1,000 Hz tone before you ever touch the sweep controls, then leave the volume alone. Because your ears are less sensitive at the frequency extremes, it's tempting to turn the volume up to "hear" 40 Hz or 18,000 Hz — doing that risks blasting your ears or your speakers the moment the sweep passes back through the midrange, where that same volume is far louder than it feels. Researchers have also looped isolated 40 Hz tones for unrelated brainwave studies, which is a completely different use case from measurement. If you want to keep a reference copy, most generators offer a wav download rather than a compressed format, since lossy compression can subtly reshape the very frequencies you're trying to measure, and higher sample rates only matter if you're sweeping near or above 20,000 Hz.

Professional Sweep Signal Measurement Considerations

Lab-grade measurement adds a few layers a casual test skips. The audio generator and analyzer need to agree on exactly when the sweep starts, so a short trigger sequence often precedes the actual test signal, giving the analyzer a fixed reference point before the real input parameter — frequency — starts changing. Systems with an automatic gain control or a level limiter need a brief pre-tone first, holding the circuit in a stable state before the sweep proper begins, since a limiter still reacting to the sweep's opening moments would quietly distort everything that follows. Once the capture is done, unwanted acoustic reflections can be trimmed out with a time window, isolating the impulse response and separating direct sound from anything bouncing off nearby surfaces. The result is plotted as an amplitude response, a phase response, or an impedance response depending on what's being measured — whether the device under test is a loudspeaker, a microphone input stage, or a full mixing console — each covering the full range within a defined measurement bandwidth, and each just a different lens on the same underlying sweep.

Run it once by ear, run it again with a proper analyzer, and you'll rarely need to guess what a room, device, or set of headphones is actually doing at any given frequency.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a frequency sweep test?
A frequency sweep test plays a tone that continuously changes from one frequency to another over a set duration. It is used to test the frequency response of speakers, headphones, amplifiers, and room acoustics by revealing how a system behaves across the audible spectrum (typically 20 Hz to 20 kHz).
What is the difference between a linear and a logarithmic sweep?
A linear sweep moves through equal Hz increments per second, spending very little time in low frequencies relative to high ones. A logarithmic sweep spends equal time per octave, which mirrors how human hearing perceives pitch. Log sweeps produce a pink-like spectrum and are generally preferred for audio equipment testing because they better match the ear's sensitivity.
Which waveform is best for a frequency sweep test?
The sine wave is the best choice for frequency response testing because it is a pure tone containing only a single frequency at any moment. Square, sawtooth, and triangle waves contain harmonics that can excite multiple frequencies simultaneously, making it harder to isolate the response at a specific frequency.
Why do sine sweeps produce better test results than pink or white noise?
Sine sweeps dedicate the full available dynamic range to one frequency at a time, delivering much higher energy per frequency than noise signals. This gives sweep tests greater immunity to background noise and allows detection of subtle resonances or distortions that broadband noise might mask.
What does 'octaves covered' mean in a frequency sweep?
An octave represents a doubling of frequency. For example, from 20 Hz to 40 Hz is one octave, and from 10 kHz to 20 kHz is also one octave. The full audible range from 20 Hz to 20 kHz spans approximately 10 octaves. Knowing octaves covered helps you understand how much of the musical and audible spectrum your sweep tests.
Can a frequency sweep damage my speakers or hearing?
Yes, playing tones at extreme volumes — especially at very low or very high frequencies — can damage both speakers and hearing. At low frequencies, speakers can be over-excursed; at high frequencies, your hearing is most fragile. Always set a safe volume level using a comfortable mid-frequency (like 1 kHz) as a reference before running a full sweep.
What is the geometric midpoint frequency in a sweep?
The geometric midpoint is the frequency halfway between the start and end frequencies on a logarithmic scale, calculated as the square root of (start × end). For a 20 Hz to 20 kHz sweep, the geometric midpoint is approximately 632 Hz — not the arithmetic midpoint of 10,010 Hz — reflecting how logarithmic perception works in audio.
What practical applications does a frequency sweep test have?
Frequency sweeps are used for speaker and headphone frequency response measurement, identifying room modes and acoustic resonances, testing amplifier distortion, calibrating audio systems, diagnosing hearing ability, and verifying that audio equipment performs within specification across the full audible range.
