Reading the Stereo Test Results

A clean pass on the stereo test means you heard a tone from the left speaker only, then a tone from the right channel only, with no bleed between the two. Good stereo separation means each channel stays cleanly isolated rather than bleeding into the other, and checking audio balance between channels matters just as much as checking raw volume. Beyond a simple left/right check, engineers also talk about stereo imaging — how precisely a system places sounds in the space between two speakers. If both channels play at once, or if pressing one button produces silence, your audio output is likely misconfigured rather than physically broken — check the operating system's sound settings before assuming a hardware fault.

A sound test is the fastest way to separate a real hardware problem from an app-specific glitch. If tones play cleanly here but a specific website or program stays silent, the problem lives in that app's mute state or volume mixer, not in your speaker placement or your sound card. Running a sound test first saves you from swapping cables or returning a perfectly good pair of speakers. Understanding Frequency Response and the Frequency Sweep Test

Frequency response describes how evenly a speaker reproduces sound across the audible range, roughly 20Hz to 20kHz — every value here is measured in hertz (Hz), the number of sound wave cycles per second. A frequency sweep test plays a continuous tone that rises from deep bass to high treble so you can hear whether your speakers reproduce that entire range evenly, or whether certain frequencies disappear or sound noticeably weaker. A full audio spectrum sweep, rather than a single tone, is the most thorough way to catch a narrow dead zone. If the tone crackles, buzzes, or distorts at any point, that's audible distortion, and it usually means a driver is being pushed past a safe volume. If you want to see the sweep visually rather than just hear it, a spectrum analyzer plots each frequency band in real time as it plays. Bass, Midrange, and Treble Explained

The audible spectrum splits into bands, and different drivers handle different parts of it. A subwoofer reproduces sub-bass and bass, a mid-range driver handles vocals and instruments, and a tweeter carries treble. The table below breaks down each band and what it sounds like during a sweep.

Using the Tone Generator for Custom Frequencies

A tone generator lets you dial in a specific frequency instead of running the full sweep — useful for finding your subwoofer's exact lower limit or checking whether your tweeter still reproduces the top end of your hearing range. Standard reference tones are 440Hz (concert pitch A) and 1000Hz, both commonly used to calibrate levels before running a sine wave generator through a full sweep.

Sine Wave Formula: From Frequency to Wavelength

Every test tone is a sine wave, and its wavelength determines how physically large a speaker driver needs to be to reproduce it efficiently. Wavelength is the speed of sound divided by frequency:

$$ \lambda = \frac{v}{f} $$

where \(v\) is the speed of sound (about 343 m/s in air) and \(f\) is the frequency in Hz. At 40Hz, the wavelength is roughly 8.6 meters — far larger than any driver, which is why reproducing sub-bass cleanly needs a big, dedicated subwoofer rather than a small satellite speaker.

Speaker Polarity Test: In Phase vs. Out of Phase

Speaker polarity refers to whether your speaker's positive and negative wires are connected the same way on both sides. Get it wrong on just one speaker and the two waveforms partially cancel instead of reinforcing each other, even though each speaker plays sound normally on its own.

How to Spot a Speaker Wired Out of Phase

Play a centered polarity test tone at moderate volume and sit equidistant between your speakers. In phase, the sound should feel focused and centered with solid bass. Out of phase, the same tone sounds wide, hollow, and noticeably thinner in the low end — the two waveforms are working against each other instead of together.

Fixing Reversed Polarity

  • Trace each speaker's positive and negative wire back to the amplifier or receiver terminal.
  • Confirm both speakers connect positive-to-positive and negative-to-negative.
  • Swap the two wires on the offending speaker only — never on both.
  • Re-run the polarity test tone to confirm the bass now sounds centered and full.

Setting Up a New Stereo Pair: A Real Polarity Check

You just swapped a decade-old bookshelf pair for a set of ELAC Debut 2.0 B6.2 speakers, rated for a 46Hz–20kHz frequency response, and before trusting them for critical listening you want to rule out a wiring mistake — this receiver's banana-plug terminals are color-reversed from your old amp, so a crossed wire on one channel is a real possibility.

You run the left/right stereo test first: left plays cleanly from the left cabinet, right from the right, so the channels themselves aren't swapped. You've set the speakers up in the standard equilateral-triangle arrangement most manufacturers recommend — 2.3 meters between cabinets, 2.3 meters back to the listening chair — so you sit dead center and switch to the polarity test tone. In phase, the tone should sound tight and centered; instead it sounds wide and strangely hollow, with almost no weight under about 150Hz, a clear sign one speaker is wired backward relative to the other.

You trace the right speaker's leads back to the terminal cup and find it: the red (positive) wire is seated in the black (negative) terminal. You swap just that one connection, leaving the left speaker untouched, and re-run the polarity tone. This time the bass locks into the center, matching the tight, focused image the spec sheet promised. With polarity confirmed, you move on to the frequency sweep and listen down to 46Hz — the pair holds up cleanly at that floor, matching the rated spec instead of falling off early the way the old set did. Satisfied the new speakers are wired correctly and performing to spec, you set the crossover on your subwoofer to the 80Hz THX-recommended handoff point and start dialing in the rest of the room.

Bass Test, Hearing Test, and Other Diagnostic Tools

Beyond the basic stereo check, a few specialized tools go deeper. A bass test isolates low frequencies to find your subwoofer's lower limit, and a hearing test maps out which frequencies you personally can and can't hear — useful context before you blame a speaker for a treble response that's actually beyond your own hearing range.

Bass Test: Finding Your Subwoofer's Lower Limit

Play a slow sweep from 20Hz upward and note where the rumble becomes audible rather than just felt. Small satellite speakers and laptop speakers often can't reproduce anything below 100Hz cleanly, which is normal and not a sign of a broken driver or bad speaker placement. A subwoofer's crossover determines the exact frequency at which it hands bass duties off to your main speakers, so don't expect a bass test to sound consistent above that point. None of this measures dynamic range — the gap between a system's quietest and loudest clean output — but a sweep that holds steady volume from top to bottom is still a good sign.

Hearing Test: How High Can You Hear?

A hearing test plays tones from roughly 250Hz up to 20kHz and asks you to note the point where sound fades out. Natural high-frequency hearing loss sets in gradually with age, so if a specific tweeter frequency seems to vanish, a quick hearing test rules out your ears before you rule out the speaker.

Headphone Test vs. This Tool

A headphone test checks the same left/right and frequency-response basics this tool does, but adds checks that don't apply to speakers — like binaural beats, which only work over headphones because each ear needs to receive a genuinely separate signal. If you're testing earbuds or headphones rather than a desktop pair, use the dedicated headphone test instead.

White Noise, Pink Noise, and the Noise Generator

Beyond single tones and sweeps, a noise generator plays a full spectrum of frequencies at once — useful for burn-in, for spotting rattles across your whole speaker placement, or for calibrating a room by ear.

White Noise vs. Pink Noise: What's the Difference

White noise carries equal energy at every frequency, which makes it sound harsh and hiss-heavy, weighted toward the treble end. Pink noise carries equal energy per octave instead, which sounds deeper and closer to how the human ear actually perceives loudness across the spectrum — it's the noise color audio engineers reach for when calibrating a sound system.

Using a Noise Generator to Calibrate Your Room

Play pink noise at a moderate, steady volume and walk around the room listening for spots where bass builds up or disappears — usually a sign of speaker placement too close to a wall or corner. A sine wave generator swept slowly through the same space can pinpoint the exact frequency where a specific room resonance shows up.

Troubleshooting: What to Do When the Sound Test Fails

This checklist covers the most common audio troubleshooting steps. Most failed speaker tests trace back to a software setting, not damaged audio hardware. Work through these checks in order before assuming the worst about your audio equipment.

No Sound at All

  • Confirm your speakers are powered on and connected to electricity, not just the audio cables.
  • Check that the correct output device is selected in your operating system's sound settings.
  • Make sure the browser tab isn't muted — look for a mute icon on the tab itself.
  • Turn the volume all the way up temporarily; weak sound can look identical to no sound at low levels.
  • If nothing works, the problem is likely an outdated or corrupted audio driver — check your sound card manufacturer's download page for the latest driver.

Only Hearing One Channel (Stereo Balance Issues)

If one channel plays and the other stays silent, check your operating system's stereo balance slider first — it's often nudged off-center by accident. If balance is centered and the issue persists, inspect the audio connectors and cabling on the silent side; in some speaker models, one speaker links to the other rather than to the source directly, and a loose link-cable will silence just that one channel.

Surround Sound Test for 5.1 and 7.1 Systems

A surround sound test extends the same left/right logic across every channel in a 5.1 or 7.1 channel setup — front, center, surround, rear, and subwoofer — so you can confirm each speaker in a home theater system is connected to the correct output and that channel balance is even across the whole room, not just between two stereo speakers or a pair of headphones.

Frequently Asked Questions

What can I use this tone generator for?
This tool lets you generate pure audio tones to test speakers and headphones for stereo balance, phase issues, and resonant frequencies. It's also useful for tuning instruments to a reference pitch, testing your hearing sensitivity across frequencies, and diagnosing audio equipment problems.
What's the difference between a Speaker Test and a Headphone Test?
A speaker test checks your room's acoustic environment along with the speaker's stereo balance and frequency response. A headphone test focuses on left/right channel isolation and in-ear frequency accuracy. Use the channel selector in this tool to isolate left or right output for either test type.
What is the safe volume level to use?
Start with a comfortable volume at a reference frequency like 1,000 Hz and avoid increasing it significantly when testing other frequencies — especially high ones where your hearing is most vulnerable. Prolonged exposure to loud tones, even if you can barely hear them, can damage hearing and equipment.
Why is the resonant frequency of a speaker important?
Every speaker has a resonant frequency (Fs) at which it vibrates most efficiently. Playing a tone at this frequency helps you identify the speaker's natural resonance point. Knowing this is essential for subwoofer box design, enclosure tuning, and ensuring your speaker isn't being driven outside its optimal range.
How do I find the resonant frequency of my speaker?
Start at a low frequency (around 20–40 Hz for subwoofers) and slowly sweep upward. The resonant frequency is where the cone excursion (movement) is greatest and you may notice a change in perceived loudness. This corresponds to the Thiele/Small parameter Fs.
What are the different waveform types and when should I use them?
Sine waves are pure tones with no harmonics — ideal for precise frequency testing. Square waves contain strong odd harmonics and stress tweeters more. Sawtooth waves have the richest harmonic content and are good for full-range speaker stress tests. Triangle waves are smoother than square waves and useful for mid-range checks.
Are the test results accurate?
The tone is generated using the Web Audio API at a sample rate of 44.1 kHz, which ensures excellent accuracy across the audible spectrum. However, actual sound output quality depends on your device's audio hardware, drivers, and speaker/headphone quality.
Can I use this speaker test on mobile?
Yes. The tool runs entirely in your browser using the Web Audio API and is compatible with modern versions of Chrome, Firefox, Safari, and Edge on both desktop and mobile. Make sure your device's volume is set to a safe level before playing any tones.
