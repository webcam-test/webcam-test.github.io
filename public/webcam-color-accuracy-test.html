Average RGB Color, Hue and Saturation

The average RGB color is the mean of every pixel's red, green and blue values on a 0-255 scale. Point the camera at something neutral, such as white paper or a grey card, and the three values should sit close together; a gap means the color balance is off. The tool converts that average into a tint angle (which way the cast leans), a strength percentage (how heavy it is), and lightness and luminosity values (how bright the scene reads).

Color Count, Bit Depth and Grayscale Mode

The number of colors is how many distinct values appear in one frame. A normal room at 720p returns tens of thousands; a reading in the hundreds means the image is flat, heavily compressed or underexposed. The tool warns about too few colors, a one-color filling (a single solid shade, usually a muted stream or a closed lens cover) and grayscale mode, which some devices use to save battery. Low bit depth acts like a low color count: gradients band and fine colour depth in skin tones disappears.

How to Test Your Webcam for Color Accuracy

A color reading is only as good as its reference. Plain white printer paper works; a grey card works better because it doesn't fluoresce under LED light. The rule of thirds grid works entirely client-side, so you get an instant result without installing an app or creating an account.

  1. Open the page in an up-to-date browser and press Test my cam.
  2. Click Allow to allow access to the webcam; the browser can't read the stream without camera permissions.
  3. With a laptop webcam and an external webcam connected, pick one in the device selector.
  4. Hold the white sheet so it fills the live preview in the webcam viewer, or sit in front of a plain wall.
  5. Keep the lighting as it will be during your call and wait a few seconds for the image to settle.
  6. Read the red, green and blue averages, change one thing (a lamp, a white balance setting) and test again.

Run the webcam test twice: under your usual light and near a window in daylight. Comparing the readings tells you whether the color accuracy problem belongs to the camera or the room.

Webcam Check Results: Spotting a Color Cast

Here's a real reading. You hold white printer paper under a warm desk lamp at 9 p.m., and the webcam test reports an average of R 222, G 205, B 176. Paper is neutral, so those three should be nearly equal. Red sits 46 points above blue: a clear orange cast, which is why faces look tanned on this setup.

The conversion uses the standard HSL formulas. The L value is the midpoint of the highest and lowest channel:

$$L = \frac{\max(R,G,B) + \min(R,G,B)}{2 \times 255} = \frac{222 + 176}{510} = 78.0\%$$

The same reading gives a tint angle of 37.8° (orange) and a strength of 41.1%, far too colorful for something white. A white balance correction scales red and blue toward green:

$$\text{gain}_R = \frac{G}{R} = \frac{205}{222} = 0.923 \qquad \text{gain}_B = \frac{G}{B} = \frac{205}{176} = 1.165$$

Switching the camera app's white balance from auto to a fixed 5000 K preset and moving the lamp behind the screen gives R 214, G 213, B 209: a 5-point spread, 5.7% strength and an image that looks natural. Luminosity, using the Rec. 709 weighting \(Y = 0.2126R + 0.7152G + 0.0722B\), rises from 206.5 to 212.9, so the fix also made the frame slightly lighter.

Other warnings point at the scene, not the sensor. If the frame is too dark, add light in front of you; if it is too bright, highlights clip and the camera can no longer auto-adjust its color.

Brightness, Exposure and Contrast Readings

The brightness figure is the plain average of the three channels: 201 in the lamp example, 212 after the fix. As a rule of thumb, readings above about 235 on a white target mean exposure is clipping, and below about 60 the camera boosts gain and adds grain. Contrast suffers at both extremes. A bright background such as a window leaves your face dark, while a dimly lit room pushes it the other way. Neither is a camera fault; a lamp in front of you fixes both.

Using a Webcam Color Accuracy Check to Sell Yarn on a Live Stream

I hand-dye yarn and sell it on a weekly live stream, and the question before each show is simple: will a skein labelled "lagoon teal" look teal on a buyer's screen, or will a return arrive a week later saying it was greener than expected? The answer this time: not until the ring light changed, because the first reading showed a 34-point warm cast on a grey card.

The setup is a 1080p USB webcam on a desk arm and a bi-colour ring light. I clip an 18% grey card where the skeins will sit, because a neutral grey of that reflectance should read close to 119 on every channel in sRGB, so it gives me a target, not just a "looks fine".

  1. Ring light at its warm 3200 K end, white balance on auto: the test reads R 131, G 118, B 97. That is a 34-point red-to-blue spread, a 37.1° tint angle and 14.9% strength. Teal under that light would be pulled toward green-grey.
  2. I work out the correction by hand: red gain 118 ÷ 131 = 0.901, blue gain 118 ÷ 97 = 1.216. A 21.6% lift on blue is more than most webcam white balance sliders give cleanly, so fixing the light beats fixing the camera.
  3. Ring light moved to 5600 K, nothing else changed: R 117, G 119, B 124, a 7-point spread and 2.9% strength, slightly cool. Rec. 709 luma barely moves (119.2 to 118.9), so the change is pure color, not brightness.

The caveat that catches most sellers: auto white balance assumes the whole frame averages to grey. Once the grey card leaves and a table of teal skeins fills the shot, the camera "corrects" the teal toward orange-grey, undoing a good reading. So after the 5600 K test, I lock white balance in the camera app at that value and re-run the test with the yarn in frame; if the grey card at the edge still reads within about 8 points across channels, the show goes ahead.

My judgement is that the light's color temperature matters far more than the webcam here. A 34-point cast came entirely from the lamp setting, and no sensor upgrade would have removed it. The number I watch before every stream is that red-to-blue spread on the grey card.

Lighting and Image Quality on Video Calls

A good result in a browser test doesn't guarantee the same picture in video meetings, because Zoom, Teams and Google Meet add their own compression and light correction. Test your webcam in the video conferencing app you actually use, through its preview or a test meeting. For most people lighting matters more than the hardware: a budget webcam under a soft front light beats a 4K one lit from behind, for streaming and online learning too.

Webcam Low-Light Performance and Image Noise

Turn off the main light and run the test again; this is the part of webcam testing most people skip. Low-light performance shows up as image noise (coloured speckle in shadows) and a drop in frame rate as the sensor slows down. Wave a hand: if it smears into motion blur, the camera is trading FPS for light. Some LED lamps also cause flicker, which the 50/60 Hz anti-flicker setting usually removes. The color reading suffers too: as gain rises, the red, green and blue averages wander and fine shades merge, so take every color reading in adequate light.

Field of View, Focus and Sharpness

Check the field of view from your normal seat, and when you take a color reading, let the white card fill the frame so a busy background doesn't skew the average. An image that stays blurry at every distance usually means a smudged lens, which also washes colors out, so clean the camera lens with a microfibre cloth first. Cameras with auto-framing re-crop as you move, which briefly shifts the color reading too.

Online Webcam Test Problems and Camera Permissions

If the online webcam test shows nothing, the cause is almost always permissions. Access can be blocked by the operating system, the browser or the site, and all three must allow it. On Windows 10 and 11, open Settings > Privacy & security > Camera and turn on access for the device and desktop apps; the Windows Camera app is a quick second check. On macOS, go to System Settings > Privacy & Security > Camera, or open Photo Booth. On Linux, Cheese confirms the webcam is detected.

Browser Settings in Chrome, Safari and Firefox

  • Chrome: click the icon left of the browser address bar, or open Settings > Privacy and security > Site settings > Camera.
  • Safari: in the browser, open Settings > Websites > Camera and set this site to Allow.
  • Firefox: click the camera icon in the browser address bar and clear the block, or open about:preferences#privacy.

Any current browser (Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari) runs the webcam test; Internet Explorer does not.

Black Screen, Drivers and Hardware Faults

  • Black screen: another app (Zoom, Teams, a recorder) is holding the camera. Close it and reload.
  • No video tracks: the camera opens but sends no frames, often a lens cover or crashed driver.
  • Outdated drivers: update them through Device Manager on Windows or the maker's software.
  • Loose connection: reseat an external camera or try another USB port.
  • Hardware failure: if the webcam also fails on a second computer, it is faulty.

Webcam Testing Tool Features and Privacy

Beyond the color readings, a good tester adds a few practical controls:

  • Mirror and natural view: mirror view is the selfie-style flip; the unflipped image is how others see you.
  • Composition grid: rule-of-thirds lines help place your eyes on the upper third.
  • Image filters: preview warmth changes, though a filter only hides a cast the webcam still records.
  • Snapshot and fullscreen: take photo of the frame as a full-resolution still and inspect it; a screenshot of the live feed loses detail.
  • Supported resolutions: every size the webcam can stream, from 640×480 up.
  • Recording: a short clip lets you hear the built-in microphone and see color in motion.

Privacy matters for any tool that opens your camera. Frequently Asked Questions

How do I test my webcam's color accuracy?
Hold plain white paper or a grey card so it fills the live preview, keep your normal lighting and read the average red, green and blue values. On a neutral target the three should be nearly equal; a gap between them is a color cast.
Why do I look pale or orange on my webcam?
Usually the light, not the camera. A warm lamp pushes red up and blue down, so skin looks orange; cool daylight or a blue screen glow does the opposite and makes you look pale. Fix the light first, then lock white balance.
What does the number of colors tell me?
It counts distinct colors in one captured frame. A normal room at 720p returns tens of thousands. A very low count points to a dark, flat or heavily compressed image, and a single color means the stream is muted or covered.
What are hue, saturation and lightness in a webcam test?
They describe the average color of the frame. Hue is which way the tint leans, saturation is how strong it is, and lightness is how bright it reads. On a white or grey target, saturation should be close to zero.
Can a webcam pass a basic test but still show poor color?
Yes. A camera can stream a sharp 1080p picture and still record a strong cast or crush shades in low light. Check color under the lighting you actually use for calls, not just whether the video appears.
Is the online color test private?
The test runs in your browser and analyses frames in memory on your device. Nothing needs to be uploaded or installed, and closing the tab discards the data.
