Identifying Rattles and Silence

Buzzing or rattling: usually a loose panel, screw, or nearby object vibrating in sympathy — not necessarily the speaker itself. Check what's near the speaker before assuming it's faulty.

Hearing nothing below ~60 Hz: very common and usually not a fault — many speakers, especially small or laptop speakers, simply can't reproduce frequencies that low at all. Silence there is a hardware limitation, not evidence something's broken.

Before you crank the volume, it helps to know exactly what you're listening for. A subwoofer test plays a series of controlled low-frequency tones through your speakers or any other audio equipment so you can hear — and sometimes feel — how far down a bass test actually reaches, whether the enclosure is tuned correctly, and whether anything rattles, distorts, or drops out along the way. The real payoff is knowing the lowest clean frequency your system can reproduce, which tells you more about real-world performance than any spec sheet.

How to Run a Subwoofer Test Safely at Home

Whether you want to test your subwoofer for the first time or double-check a repair, the process is the same. Start a sweep down through the full low frequency range, or step through fixed tones — 20Hz, 40Hz, 80Hz — using bass presets on any online subwoofer test or tone generator tool. Most tools sweep from around 150Hz down toward 10-20Hz. Before you begin, disconnect or mute your main speakers so you're only listening to the subwoofer; this isolates its audio output and makes any rattle far easier to pinpoint.

Setting a Safe Starting Level

Start low. High sound pressure level at extreme low frequencies can damage speakers and drivers before you notice it happening — ease into the test rather than jumping straight to full output.

Increase the level gradually rather than jumping straight to reference loudness. Very low frequencies need significantly more amplifier headroom to sound as loud as midrange content, so it's easy to overdrive a driver at 20Hz that would sound comfortable at 200Hz. If you hear buzzing or a sudden change in tone, back off immediately — that's usually cone excursion exceeding a safe limit, not a normal part of the sweep.

  • Confirm your subwoofer and amplifier are wired correctly before testing
  • Start well below your usual listening level
  • Isolate the subwoofer from your main speakers or full-range channels
  • Listen for rattling, buzzing, or a sudden drop in output as you sweep down
  • Note the lowest frequency that still sounds clean and controlled
  • Confirm your enclosure tuning frequency and box tuning match your driver's specs

Sub-Bass vs Mid-Bass: What Each Range Sounds Like

These two ranges are worth separating out because they stress a subwoofer very differently, and most listeners can tell them apart once they've heard both side by side in the same sweep.

Sub-Bass (10–30 Hz)

This range sits at the edge of the human hearing range. Below roughly 20Hz you're mostly feeling vibration in your chest and the floor rather than hearing a musical pitch — this is subsonic, near-infrasound territory that needs a large driver and correct enclosure tuning to reproduce without excessive distortion.

The 60–100 Hz Punch Range

This range carries the punch of kick drums, the low end of bass guitars and vocals, and most of what listeners simply call "bass" in everyday music. Most small speakers handle it reasonably well; it's the octave below that separates a real subwoofer from a bookshelf speaker.

What a Frequency Sweep Test Reveals About Your Subwoofer

A frequency sweep test is the fastest way to map a subwoofer's usable range from top to bottom in a single pass, rather than guessing at individual frequencies. Each tone in the sweep is a pure sine tone at a fixed frequency, which is what makes it useful for diagnostics: a sine wave doesn't contain any of the harmonics that make music or speech recognizable, so any extra tone you hear is coming from your system, not the source file.

Low frequencies also behave differently in physical space, which is worth understanding before you judge the result. Wavelength and frequency are directly related by the speed of sound:

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f}$$

where v is the speed of sound (about 343 m/s) and f is frequency in Hz. At 40Hz, that works out to a wavelength of roughly 8.6 meters — larger than most rooms. That's why very low frequencies sound nearly the same no matter where you stand in a room, and why the room itself, not your ears, decides how a sweep actually sounds at a given seat: standing waves can boost or cancel specific frequencies by several decibels depending on where the subwoofer and listener sit.

Reading Frequency Response and Roll-Off

A clean frequency response should sound roughly consistent in loudness as you sweep down, until you hit the driver's natural roll-off point, where output falls away quickly. A gentle, gradual roll-off is normal; a sudden drop, buzz, or total silence in the middle of the sweep usually points to a port, wiring, or amp problem rather than a genuine limit of the driver.

Diagnosing Rattles and Harmonic Distortion During a Bass Test

Distortion is the most useful thing this kind of test can catch, because it's rarely audible during normal listening until you isolate a single frequency and turn the level up. Harmonic distortion adds extra tones — usually at whole-number multiples of the original frequency, an octave or more above it — that weren't in the original signal, and it gets worse as cone excursion increases.

Common Sources of Cabin and Room Rattle

This kind of rattle during a bass test almost always comes from something physical resonating in sympathy with a specific frequency, not the subwoofer itself failing. Loose trim panels, unsecured enclosure screws, items on a nearby shelf, and even loose window glass can all buzz at one particular tone and go quiet everywhere else. A well-built sound system should stay quiet at every frequency in the sweep, with total harmonic distortion low enough that you can't consciously hear it — audio engineers generally treat anything under about 10% THD as acceptable audio quality for a loudspeaker, and a well-matched subwoofer and amp should sit well below that at normal listening levels.

Confirming a New Enclosure Before a Long Drive

You've just built a 0.87 cubic foot sealed enclosure for a 12-inch subwoofer and wired it to the factory amplifier, and there's a six-hour drive planned for the weekend. The driver's spec sheet lists a -3dB point of 28Hz in a sealed box of roughly that volume, so that's the number you're checking against — not whether it sounds loud in the driveway.

You disconnect the front and rear door speakers at the head unit so only the subwoofer channel is active, and set the amplifier's gain to about two-thirds of its travel, leaving headroom so a 20Hz tone won't clip the input before it ever reaches the cone. Starting the sweep at 100Hz, output stays even and controlled through 60Hz and 40Hz. At 34Hz, a faint buzz appears that wasn't there a few hertz earlier, and it gets louder at 32Hz.

Rather than assume the driver is faulty, you press a hand against the trunk latch while the tone plays — the buzz stops, then returns once you let go. The latch striker is resonating at that specific frequency, not the subwoofer itself. A strip of felt tape on the striker plate and a re-run of the same sweep from 100Hz down to 20Hz confirms it: the rattle at 32-34Hz is gone, and the enclosure holds clean output down to 26Hz before it rolls off — two hertz below the printed -3dB spec, well within a reasonable margin for a hand-built box.

With that confirmed, you leave the amplifier gain where it is rather than pushing it higher. A fully loaded trunk for the trip will damp the sealed enclosure's response slightly anyway, so there's no reason to add extra bass boost chasing output the box was never going to lose.

Improving Bass Response After a Low Frequency Test

Once you know a subwoofer's usable range and where distortion creeps in, a few practical changes usually improve output more than buying new equipment.

Speaker Placement and Room Acoustics

Speaker Placement

Corner placement reinforces low end by using two room boundaries instead of one, which is why the classic "subwoofer crawl" technique — placing the sub at your listening position and crawling around the room to find where it sounds best, then putting the subwoofer there instead — still works. Avoid pushing a subwoofer flush against a wall unless you specifically want emphasized low end.

Room Acoustics

This affects low end far more than it affects midrange or treble. Speaker drivers alone don't determine how a room sounds — standing waves between parallel walls can make one frequency boom and another vanish almost entirely a few feet away, so it's worth re-running the test from your actual listening seat, not just next to the speaker.

EQ, Bass Boost, and Amp Headroom

A modest equalizer boost in the 40-100Hz range can help a thin-sounding system, but a heavy-handed bass boost setting is one of the fastest ways to introduce distortion and muddy the sound. Give your amp enough headroom that it isn't clipping at the level you actually listen at — clipping at low frequencies is a common cause of driver damage over time.

Headphones and Earbuds

The same logic applies at a smaller scale. Premium over-ear headphones can often reach down to 20Hz cleanly, while budget earbuds typically roll off somewhere between 40-60Hz — run the same test tones through both and the difference in output is usually obvious within the first few seconds.

Car Audio vs Home Theater: Choosing the Right Subwoofer Testing Method

The way you interpret results depends heavily on the environment. In car audio, cabin gain (the way a small, sealed space naturally reinforces low frequencies) means an enclosure tuned for a living room can sound boomy and overdamped once it's installed behind a back seat — a professional installer will often re-tune the box or adjust the amp's low-pass filter after listening to how the vehicle's own cabin changes the result. In a home theater system, you have far more room volume to fill, so driver size and enclosure design matter more, and correct placement does more of the work that cabin gain handles for free in a car.

Whichever environment you're testing in, a subwoofer sound test only tells you something useful if you run it consistently: same level reference, same seating position, and the same sequence of test tones every time. Bass enthusiasts who treat every sound test as a checkpoint — after a new enclosure, a different amp, or simply moving a couch — get far more value out of it than someone running it once and guessing at what the numbers mean.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a bass test and how does it work?
A bass test plays low-frequency tones (typically 10–200 Hz) through your speakers or headphones to check how well your audio equipment reproduces deep bass sounds. By listening at various frequencies, you can identify where your speakers start to roll off and lose low-frequency response. This helps you understand the real-world performance of your subwoofer, headphones, or speakers beyond what spec sheets say.
How low can my speakers or headphones go?
Most consumer headphones can reproduce frequencies down to about 20 Hz, but the quality varies significantly. Laptop and phone speakers typically cut off around 80–100 Hz. A dedicated subwoofer can often reach 20 Hz or even lower. To find your equipment's limit, slowly reduce the frequency from 100 Hz downward until you can no longer hear or feel the tone.
Should I feel bass or hear it?
Both are valid — it depends on the frequency. Very low sub-bass frequencies (10–30 Hz) are more felt than heard, creating physical vibration and pressure in your chest and room. Frequencies from 30–60 Hz are the classic 'deep bass' you feel and hear. Above 60 Hz, bass becomes primarily an auditory experience with warmth and punch rather than physical vibration.
Can bass damage my speakers or headphones?
Yes, playing very loud bass tones at extreme low frequencies can damage speakers and headphones. High sound pressure levels (SPL) cause large cone excursions that can blow speaker drivers. Always start at low volume (20–30%) and increase gradually. Be especially cautious with sub-bass frequencies below 30 Hz, which can stress speaker components even at moderate volumes.
Why can't I hear very low frequencies below 30 Hz?
Human hearing typically begins at around 20 Hz, but sensitivity at extreme low frequencies is much lower than at mid-range. Very low frequencies (10–30 Hz) require significantly more energy to perceive, and most audio equipment cannot reproduce them at usable levels. Additionally, room acoustics and headphone seal quality heavily influence whether you can hear or feel these frequencies.
What's a good lowest frequency for headphones?
A quality pair of over-ear or in-ear headphones should comfortably reproduce frequencies down to 20–30 Hz. Premium audiophile headphones may extend below 20 Hz, though this approaches the limits of human hearing. Open-back headphones often struggle at very low frequencies compared to closed-back designs, which provide better bass seal and extension.
How do I test my subwoofer with this tool?
Set the frequency to 80 Hz to start, then gradually lower it toward 20 Hz in steps. At each frequency, listen and feel whether the subwoofer produces output. Use the sweep mode to automatically scan the frequency range. If your subwoofer cuts out or sounds distorted before reaching 30 Hz, it may have a limited low-frequency extension or need repositioning for better room coupling.
Does speaker placement affect bass response?
Absolutely. Placing a subwoofer in a corner of the room reinforces bass output significantly (sometimes by 6–12 dB) because room boundaries act as reflectors. Room dimensions also create standing waves and bass resonances at specific frequencies. Experimenting with subwoofer placement — against walls, in corners, or away from boundaries — can dramatically change how much bass you hear and feel.
