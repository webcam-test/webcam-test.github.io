Can anyone actually hear you? A microphone test answers that in ten seconds: speak, watch the real-time meter move, then listen back on headphones before a call, podcast or streaming session. This online check gives you a clear pass or fail plus the audio readings behind it.

How to Test Your Microphone in Your Browser

On a laptop, desktop computer or smartphone, the browser microphone test runs locally with no software to install, a free online tool to open before any call:

  1. Click Start on the widget above.
  2. When the browser asks, click Allow so the page can use your mic.
  3. Pick your mic from the list if more than one appears.
  4. Speak a sentence at normal level and watch the meter.
  5. Record a short clip, then play it back.

Any recent version of Chrome, Firefox, Safari or Edge supports what this online mic test needs, on Windows, Android and iOS alike.

Allow Microphone Access and Choose the Input Device

Your browser blocks microphone access until you grant permission, and it remembers that choice per site. Click Block by mistake and the meter stays flat. Once access is granted, the device selector lists every mic the system can see: a headset, a webcam with a built-in microphone or the laptop's own array. With multiple microphones connected, the page may open on the default device, so pick the right entry first.

Read the Waveform and Visual Feedback

The moving waveform is the quickest way to check microphone function: if the sound waves jump when you speak and settle when you stop, the mic is live. A level meter (or visualizer bars) adds scale: normal speech should push the volume levels into the upper middle, well short of the top. A barely flickering trace means a switched-off or distant mic; one that hits the end on every word means the input is too hot.

Recording and Playback: Test My Mic by Ear

To hear what other people hear, record a 10-second clip and play it back. Use headphones for live monitoring, because routing your mic to your speakers creates a feedback loop. Listen for hum, a hollow room sound, or words that break up. You can download the audio clip as an MP3 or a lossless WAV; browser recordings are usually mono with a 16 bit depth, plenty for a voice check.

What the Format Readings Mean

The sample rate sets the highest pitch the mic can capture, half the rate: 48,000 Hz covers up to 24 kHz, past the 20Hz–20kHz range of human hearing. Sample size sets how finely each snapshot is measured; 16 bits is the norm for calls and 24 bits for studio recording. Audio channels reads 1 for almost any speech mic.

What the Processing Flags Do

Your browser applies three filters by default. Echo cancellation strips sound from your own speakers out of the mic feed, so the other side doesn't hear itself repeated. Noise suppression removes steady sounds such as fans and air conditioning. Automatic gain control rides your level up and down. All three help two-way communication but colour a clip, so if yours sounds pumping or underwater, these filters are the first suspect; switch them off in the recording app.

Input Level, Latency and Signal-to-Noise Ratio

Levels are read in dBFS (decibels relative to full scale), where 0 is the ceiling:

$$\text{dBFS} = 20 \log_{10}(A)$$

Here \(A\) is the sample amplitude, from 0 to 1. In the sample report, a speaking peak of \(A = 0.355\) gives −9.0 dBFS, and the quiet gap between words sits at \(A = 0.00089\), or −61.0 dBFS. The signal-to-noise ratio is the difference between the two:

$$\text{SNR} = -9.0 - (-61.0) = 52\ \text{dB}$$

A gap that wide keeps hiss and room tone far beneath your voice; part of that quiet floor is the mic's own self-noise. Peaks between roughly −18 and −6 dBFS leave headroom, while anything touching 0 dBFS causes clipping, a hard distortion no filter can undo.

Delay follows its own formula:

$$\text{latency} = \frac{\text{buffer size}}{\text{samples per second}} = \frac{1024}{48{,}000} = 21.3\ \text{ms}$$

Under about 30 ms, you won't notice the lag while monitoring yourself.

A Microphone Check Before a Remote Job Interview

A job candidate has a panel interview on Microsoft Teams in 40 minutes and one decision to make: speak through her laptop's built-in mic, or dig out the wired headset from a drawer. She runs both through the mic check and reads the numbers rather than trusting her ears. The answer comes back clearly: the headset wins, with a 47.8 dB gap between speech and the quiet floor against the laptop's 25.7 dB.

She starts with the laptop, sitting at her usual 35 cm from the screen, and reads her opening line ("I've spent six years in supply-chain analytics") twice. The peak amplitude settles at 0.081, which is \(20 \log_{10}(0.081)\) = −21.8 dBFS, below the −18 dBFS alignment level that EBU R 68 uses as a broadcast reference. The pause between sentences sits at 0.0042, or −47.5 dBFS, mostly the laptop's own fan.

Then she plugs in the headset, its boom about 4 cm from the corner of her mouth, and repeats the same line:

Laptop mic, fan running−21.8 dBFS−47.5 dBFS25.7 dB
Laptop mic, fan throttled−21.8 dBFS−54.4 dBFS32.6 dB

She checks the middle row on purpose: closing a browser tab full of video drops the fan, and the floor falls 6.9 dB. That helps, but the laptop still loses by 15.2 dB, so the fan was never the main problem. Distance was.

What I'd flag here is the gap between theory and the reading. Going from 35 cm to 4 cm should add \(20 \log_{10}(35 \div 4)\) = 18.8 dB, yet her peak only rose 10.4 dB. The laptop's automatic gain control had already boosted her quiet speech before the meter saw it, which flatters the laptop's peak and lifts its floor at the same time. That's why the test's peak reading on its own can mislead you: with gain riding, compare the gap between the meter's two readings, not the loudest moment, and read it after the first two or three seconds once the gain has settled.

Her next step is concrete: headset in, Teams input switched to it by name rather than "Default", and one 15-second recording played back to confirm the −11.4 dBFS peak isn't clipping on louder words. If the interview runs long and she has to move to the laptop, she now knows to lean in to about 20 cm and kill the video tab first.

Microphone Not Working? Fixes After a Microphone Check

When the meter stays flat, the usual culprit isn't the mic at all: Zoom, Discord, Microsoft Teams or Google Meet can hold exclusive control, so close those apps first. Then follow this troubleshooting order and test your microphone again after each fix.

Browser Permission and Privacy Settings

In Chrome, click the icon left of the address bar and set the mic to Allow; in Firefox, clear the block in the same panel. The operating system can still block it: Windows and macOS privacy settings decide which apps may use the mic, overriding anything you allow in the page. On Linux, check the input source in your sound server.

Sound Settings, Input Volume and Device Manager

  • On Windows 11, open Settings, then System, then Sound, and pick the mic under Input.
  • On a Mac, open System Settings, then Sound, then Input.
  • Make sure the mic isn't muted and the slider isn't near zero: a mic at 5% passes but sounds distant.
  • Check for a physical mute button, often on the cord.
  • If the mic doesn't show up, open Device Manager, update or reinstall the audio drivers, then restart; on a Mac, restarting Core Audio clears a stuck input.

Cable, USB Port and Hardware Failure Checks

Reseat the plug, try a different USB port or swap in a second audio jack lead. Crackle or static that changes when you move the cable points to a damaged lead. Bluetooth headsets drop to a low-quality call mode while the mic is active, so test wired if you can. If it fails on a second computer too, the unit is faulty.

Types of Microphone Testing for Better Sound Quality

These checks test microphone performance, not just function. Run them in a quiet environment, twice each, and open clips in Audacity or other recording software that shows a spectrum.

Background Noise and Frequency Response Tests

Record ten seconds of silence, then ten of speech, and compare. The silent part reveals background noise (fans, traffic, keyboard clicks) and the ambient noise of a bare room. Room acoustics matter as much as the mic: soft furnishings cut the reflections that make speech sound hollow. Frequency response describes which pitches a mic favours; a speech mic often lifts the upper mids for clarity, which you hear on playback.

Distance and Polar Pattern Tests

Your level changes with distance by the inverse-square rule:

$$\Delta L = 20 \log_{10}\left(\frac{d_1}{d_2}\right)$$

To run it with the test, record the same line at 25 cm and then at 8 cm: the peak dBFS reading should rise by about \(20 \log_{10}(25 \div 8)\) = +9.9 dB, and that close you'll also hear the proximity effect, a bass boost. Keep a consistent distance once the meter peaks in the target band, and use a pop filter to tame plosives. To check the polar pattern, watch the meter as you talk from the front, side and behind: a cardioid mic's peak drops sharply at the back; an omnidirectional one reads the same all round.

Why Use a Mic Test Tool Before Meetings

A ten-second check before meetings, video calls or a webinar spares you the "can you hear me?" minute at the start, and it catches the wrong input before a podcast recording you can't redo. Whenever you check mic levels on a new setup, test your mic again after every change.

Choosing a USB or XLR Microphone for Better Audio Quality

If the test exposes a weak mic, a USB mic plugs straight in and is the easy route for content creation. An XLR mic needs an audio interface (and phantom power for a condenser) but gives more control. Let the test results pick the type: if your silent recording showed a high floor, dynamic mics reject more room sound and handle high SPL; if your clip sounded dull, condensers have higher sensitivity and catch more detail. For a home office, a dynamic model is often the better audio quality pick.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is my audio sent anywhere during the mic test?
No. The test runs in your browser, and any clip you record stays on your device unless you download and share it yourself.
Do I need to install software to test my microphone?
No. A recent version of Chrome, Firefox, Safari or Edge is enough. You only have to allow microphone access when the browser asks.
Can I test a microphone on my phone?
Yes. Mobile browsers on Android and iOS support the test. Keep the page in the foreground and don't lock the screen, or the browser may pause the mic.
Why is my recording too quiet or too loud?
Your input level is set too low or too high. Adjust it in your system sound settings, keep a steady distance from the mic, and aim for peaks between -18 and -6 dBFS.
Why do I hear an echo during the test?
Your speakers are feeding sound back into the mic. Use headphones when you listen to yourself, and keep echo cancellation switched on for calls.
How do I test my mic on Windows 11?
Open Settings, then System, then Sound, choose your microphone under Input and speak; the input volume bar should move as you talk.
Why does the test say my microphone is in use or blocked?
Another app such as Zoom, Teams or Discord may be holding the mic, or the browser or operating system has blocked access. Close the other app, then check the site permission and your privacy settings.
