What This Actually Measures

Every click you make measures your reaction time plus audio latency combined — a browser can't separate the two. This test subtracts a typical average human auditory reaction time (~190 ms) from your average, and reports the result as a range to reflect that real uncertainty, rather than a falsely precise single number.

Where your browser reports it, AudioContext.baseLatency and outputLatency give a partial, hardware-only figure alongside your reaction-based estimate — useful context, though it doesn't capture your full output device chain (e.g. Bluetooth's own added delay).

How to Run This Audio Latency Test

Put on the headphones or speakers you actually use day to day, turn the volume to a comfortable level, and watch the screen while you listen. What You'll Need Before You Start

You don't need any special equipment: a browser tab, your normal audio output, and a quiet-ish room are enough. If you'd rather judge timing by ear alone without watching the screen, some simpler testers skip straight to a tap-along method instead, having you match a beat at a fixed BPM and comparing your taps against a trailing average of your last several hits — a rougher approach, but one that needs nothing more than your own reflexes.

Reading Your Output Latency Result

Once the flash and click feel locked together, the slider's position is your result. That number is specific to this exact chain — this device, this connection, these headphones — so don't assume it carries over to a different pair of earbuds or a different laptop.

What Output Latency Numbers Actually Mean

Every device you own adds its own delay between "sound was triggered" and "sound left the speaker." This test reports the combined result, and understanding why helps you interpret what you're seeing rather than just staring at a number. The result you get approximates your true audio output latency, not a single isolated component of it. The frequency sweep test is free to use with no sign-up, and works on both desktop and mobile browsers.

The Formula Behind Your Measurement

Professional tools measure the same thing more precisely by playing a sine sweep through a loopback cable connected from your audio interface's output back into its input, then cross-correlating the recording against the original signal. A serious audio interface usually exposes ASIO or WASAPI drivers rather than the generic consumer stack, and running the device in full-duplex mode lets you play and record in a single pass. The relationship between the raw sample count and the time it represents is simple:

$$ \text{Latency (ms)} = \frac{\text{Samples} \times 1000}{\text{Sample Rate (Hz)}} $$

At 96 kHz, 221 samples of round-trip delay works out to roughly 2.3 ms — the kind of figure dedicated hardware with a short DAC stage can hit, well below what this browser-based test will show you on typical consumer hardware.

Sample Rate and Buffer Size Explained

Two settings do most of the work in determining your number. A larger buffer size trades responsiveness for stability — less headroom, but more delay — while a higher sample rate can shrink the time each buffer represents. Every stage in your device's processing chain, from the app down to the driver, adds its own slice on top of whatever the buffer already cost you, and each layer of that audio stack is invisible from the outside — exactly why a direct measurement like this beats guessing.

Bluetooth Latency Test Results vs. Wired Audio

If you're testing Bluetooth headphones, start over a wired connection first to establish your baseline reaction time, then switch to Bluetooth so the gap between the two readings isolates the actual wireless delay. Bluetooth latency depends heavily on which codec your phone and headphones negotiate, and that choice alone can swing your result by hundreds of milliseconds.

Round-Trip Latency and Why It's a Lower Bound

Round-trip latency measured over a loopback cable is always a touch lower than what you'll perceive with real headphones and speakers, because it skips the acoustic path entirely. That's also why lip-sync problems on a TV or soundbar can appear even when a manufacturer's spec sheet looks fine on paper — the spec was measured under lab conditions your living room doesn't share.

A Vocalist Decides Between Bluetooth and Wired Monitoring

Mara is tracking a vocal take for a demo at 11:40 p.m., after her housemates are asleep, so she's monitoring through her AirPods Pro 2 instead of the studio monitors she'd normally use. Before hitting record, she wants to know whether what she's hearing through the earbuds is close enough to real time to sing in tune and in the pocket, or whether the delay will throw her off without her realizing it.

She opens the test on her laptop, keeps the AirPods connected exactly as they are for the session — same Bluetooth link, same volume — and works the slider until the click and flash lock together. It settles at 132.6 ms, consistent with the AAC codec her MacBook and the AirPods negotiate rather than a faster low-latency profile. She runs it twice more to be sure: 129.8 ms, then 134.1 ms, close enough to call it stable.

That number is well past the roughly 20 ms ceiling audio engineers use for real-time monitoring, the point past which a singer starts unconsciously dragging behind the beat to compensate for what they're hearing. Anything under it and the ear can't separate the delay from normal room acoustics; anything meaningfully over it, and pitch and timing both start to drift without an obvious cause.

Mara unplugs the AirPods and switches to her audio interface's wired headphone output, then reruns the same test. It settles at 6.2 ms — inside the range where monitoring feels direct rather than a fraction behind. She keeps the wired connection for the actual take, and makes a note on the session file to always re-run this check before tracking anything timing-sensitive on a new pair of headphones, rather than assuming last week's result still holds.

How to Measure Audio Latency for Your Use Case

There's no single "good" number — what counts as acceptable depends entirely on what you're doing with the sound. Once you know how to measure audio latency for your own setup, you can hold the result up against the threshold that actually matters for your situation instead of guessing.

Fast-Paced Games and Video Sync Thresholds

For fast-paced games, once you're consistently under roughly 60 ms your inputs start to feel responsive rather than mushy. Video is a little more forgiving up to a point — lip-sync errors tend to become noticeable somewhere around 80–100 ms, which is exactly where mid-tier Bluetooth codecs tend to land.

Live Performance and In-Ear Monitoring

For live performance or singing through in-ear monitors, you want to stay under roughly 20 ms — beyond that, the delay starts to audibly interfere with your own timing, since part of what you hear reaches your inner ear directly through bone conduction anyway. Musicians and a sound engineer mixing monitors from front-of-house both feel this threshold the same way: cross it, and performers start fighting the mix instead of playing with it.

Diagnosing Latency Tester Warnings on Windows

On Windows, latency problems usually trace back to something else on the system competing for the audio device's attention. Windows itself is not a strict real-time audio operating system — it delivers requests on a best-effort basis, which is normally fine but occasionally isn't, and that's exactly what a monitoring utility is built to expose.

What Causes Dropouts, Clicks and Pops

Under the hood, the system schedules audio work through an interrupt service routine (ISR) and a matching DPC that offloads the rest of the work. If either one takes too long to finish, you'll hear it as dropouts, clicks and pops rather than see it reflected anywhere in this test's number.

Kernel Timers, DPCs, and Hard Pagefaults

A kernel timer measurement shows how consistently those routines actually complete over time. If the delay ever crosses into a hard pagefault — the system reaching out to disk for something that should have stayed in memory — a monitoring utility will flag exactly which process caused the underrun so you're not left guessing.

  • Close background programs that might be competing for the sound card
  • Raise your device's buffer a notch if you hear glitches
  • Match your device's sampling rate to whatever this test and your other audio software expect
  • Confirm your ASIO settings match your hardware before re-measuring
  • Re-run the test after each change instead of guessing whether it helped

Latency Tester Setups for Live Sound and Streaming Rigs

Wireless monitoring adds its own layer on top of whatever your device already contributes, which is why it's worth testing the whole chain rather than just your headphones in isolation.

Digital Audio Consoles and Wi-Fi Routers

A typical wireless monitoring rig connects a digital audio console to a dedicated WiFi router, then streams uncompressed stereo audio to each performer's phone, where they build their own personal mix independent of everyone else on stage. Consumer routers weren't designed for this kind of consistent low-latency traffic, which is exactly why a dedicated router matters more than most people expect.

In-Ear Monitoring for Musicians and Sound Engineers

When you're building a streaming mix for rehearsal or a live stream, treat your baseline reading as the floor, not the whole story — it's always a little lower than what performers perceive once real acoustics and real headphones are involved. Re-check it any time you change routers, encoders, or headphones, the same way you'd re-check a recording after adjusting any other setting in the chain.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is audio latency?
Audio latency is the delay between when a sound is triggered and when you actually hear it through your output device. It is measured in milliseconds (ms). Lower latency is better, especially for live instruments, gaming, and professional audio production.
How does this audio latency test work?
Enter your BPM and connection type. The tool calculates the millisecond gap between each beat at that tempo, then compares your connection's typical latency against that window. If you have a measured latency figure, you can enter it to see how it stacks up against your connection type's expected range.
How much latency is acceptable?
For professional audio production and live instruments, 10 ms or lower is ideal. For gaming and video, under 80 ms is generally acceptable. For casual music listening, even 150–300 ms (common with SBC Bluetooth) is tolerable. The acceptable threshold depends heavily on the use case.
Why is Bluetooth audio latency so high?
Bluetooth audio introduces latency due to wireless encoding, decoding, and buffering processes. Older codecs like SBC can add 150–300 ms, while newer codecs like aptX Adaptive or LE Audio have reduced this to 40–80 ms. Wired connections bypass this entirely and typically measure under 20 ms.
What does 'milliseconds per beat' mean in this context?
At any given BPM, each beat spans a fixed number of milliseconds (ms per beat = 60,000 ÷ BPM). If your audio latency is a large fraction of that window, you will perceive a noticeable delay between a trigger and the sound — especially critical at higher tempos.
What is the difference between output latency and round-trip latency?
Output latency measures the delay from triggering a sound to hearing it. Round-trip latency includes both output and input (microphone capture), measuring the full loop. Round-trip latency is what tools like the Superpowered Web Latency Test measure using a speaker-to-microphone beep test.
What BPM should I use for the latency test?
Use the BPM of the music or metronome you are working with. A common reference is 120 BPM (500 ms per beat). Higher tempos make latency more perceptible because the ms window per beat is smaller, so even moderate delays become a noticeable fraction of the beat.
Which connection type has the lowest audio latency?
Wired connections (3.5mm jack or USB audio) consistently offer the lowest latency, typically 5–20 ms. Among Bluetooth codecs, aptX Adaptive and LE Audio are the best, ranging from 40–80 ms. Older SBC Bluetooth is the worst, often adding 150–300 ms of delay.
