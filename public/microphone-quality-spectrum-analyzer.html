Real-Time FFT Spectrum Explained

The FFT slices your audio into thousands of overlapping time windows and reports the amplitude present at each frequency bin, so the FFT spectrum you see is really a snapshot updated many times a second rather than a single static reading. A wider window smooths out jitter; a narrower one reacts faster to a plucked string or a slammed door — the same trade-off engineers weigh during any live spectrum analysis session.

Frequency Resolution and FFT Size

Every spectrum analyser has to trade speed for detail, and the dial that controls that trade-off is FFT size. Frequency resolution — how close together two tones can sit before they blur into one bar — is set by a simple relationship:

$$ \Delta f = \frac{\text{Sample Rate}}{\text{FFT Size}} $$

Set the sample rate to 48,000 Hz with an FFT size of 8192, and each bin covers roughly 5.9 Hz — plenty for spotting a stray 60 Hz hum but too coarse to separate two vocal harmonics a semitone apart. Push the FFT size to 16384 at that same rate and resolution tightens to under 3 Hz per bin, matching the sharper readings a dedicated frequency analyser advertises. The cost is latency: a bigger FFT window takes longer to fill, so the display lags a beat behind what you just played.

Once audio is flowing, you get far more than one number — a full curve across the audible range, plus a handful of summary stats worth learning to read at a glance.

Peak Frequency, RMS, and Crest Factor

Alongside the curve, the analyzer tracks the peak frequency — the single loudest bin in the current window — and reports it in Hz so you can see exactly what's dominating the signal. Two related stats round out the picture: RMS measures average power and tracks how loud something actually sounds, while Peak captures the single highest sample. The gap between them is the crest factor; a wide gap means dynamic, uncompressed audio, and a narrow one means the signal has been squashed hard by a limiter or heavy compression.

Spectrogram vs Spectrum View

The standard spectrum view shows one instant — frequency on one axis, amplitude on the other. Switch to the spectrogram and you get a scrolling, color-coded map with time running left to right and frequency running bottom to top, so brightness at a given height tells you how strong that frequency was at that moment. It's the better view for anything that changes over time: a room mode that rings after a clap, a vibrato held on a sustained note, or a hum that only appears when a nearby amp is switched on. Reading a spectrogram takes a little practice, but once a pattern like a steady hum's flat horizontal line becomes familiar, you'll pick it out instantly in every session after.

Frequency Response, Octave Bands, and Room Acoustics

Beyond a single spectrum snapshot, this tool doubles as a lightweight way to check frequency response — how evenly a microphone, speaker, or room treats every part of the audible range — without renting calibrated measurement gear.

A/C/Z Weighting Explained

Flip between A/C/Z weighting and you're changing how the meter mimics human hearing. Z is flat, treating every frequency equally — useful for raw diagnostics. A rolls off bass and extreme treble to approximate how loud a sound feels to your ears, which is why it's the standard for environmental and workplace noise limits. C stays flatter at low frequencies and better tracks peak, impact-style noise where bass content genuinely matters for safety limits, not just perception.

Measuring Rooms with Test Tones

Octave bands group the spectrum into bands of doubling frequency rather than a raw FFT bin, which is the format most acoustic engineering and building-noise standards actually use. Play pink noise — equal energy per octave, the standard reference signal for room and speaker measurement — through your monitors, capture it with the mic, and any band that spikes or dips well past its neighbors points to a room mode or absorption problem worth treating physically rather than fixing in software.

Test Signals: Pink Noise, White Noise, and Sine Sweeps

A built-in test signal generator is worth having next to the analyzer. White noise, with equal energy per Hz rather than per octave, is better suited to system stress tests than room measurement. A sine wave at a single chosen frequency is handy for calibration or for hearing exactly what one band sounds like in isolation. A log sweep — a tone that glides from 20 Hz to 20 kHz over several seconds — lets you watch resonances and rolloff appear on the spectrum in real time as it passes through them. Always lower your monitor volume before playing any of these; a full-level sweep can damage tweeters and ears alike.

Detecting Mains Hum and Electrical Noise

Mains hum shows up as a narrow, unwavering vertical spike at 50 Hz or 60 Hz depending on your country's power grid, often with fainter harmonics at 100/150/200 Hz or 120/180/240 Hz stacked above it. Because it never moves and never fades the way music or speech does, it's one of the easiest problems to confirm visually — a quick glance at the spectrum tells you in seconds whether a ground loop, an unbalanced cable, or a nearby dimmer switch is the culprit, long before you'd notice it by ear alone in a busy room.

Note Detection, Harmonics, and Musical Analysis

Watching the analyzer lock onto a pitch is one of the more satisfying features here: sing or play a note, and it reports the strongest energy in the spectrum as a musical pitch rather than a raw frequency number.

How Nearest Musical Note Detection Works

The tool finds the highest-energy bin, maps it to the nearest musical note using A4 = 440 Hz as the reference pitch, and shows the cent deviation — how many cents sharp or flat you are from that note's ideal frequency. A well-built detector uses a harmonic product spectrum approach rather than picking the single loudest peak, so it locks onto the true fundamental instead of getting fooled by a louder overtone one octave up.

Fundamental Frequency vs Harmonics

Every pitched sound is really a stack: a fundamental frequency plus a series of quieter harmonic partials sitting at whole-number multiples above it, and that mix of harmonics is what makes a violin sound different from a trumpet playing the identical note. On the spectrum, the fundamental is usually — but not always — the tallest bar, with a descending staircase of harmonics trailing off toward the treble end.

Checking a Hotel Room Before a Remote Interview

You're recording a remote guest for a client's podcast from a hotel room, and the guest is wired with a lavalier mic clipped 18.4 cm below the chin, angled slightly off-axis to avoid breath pops. Before the guest even sits down, you want to know whether the room itself is quiet enough to cut cleanly, so you set the analyzer's weighting to A (matching how a listener actually perceives loudness), pick a 30-second Leq window, and just let the empty room sit in front of the mic while the HVAC runs normally.

The session comes back with Leq at -54.2 dBFS, L10 at -49.6 dBFS, and L90 at -58.7 dBFS — the quiet floor underneath the intermittent hum of the air handler. You then have the guest read a line at a normal conversational level and watch the live spectrum peak around -22.4 dBFS. That puts roughly 36 dB between the guest's speaking level and the room's L90 floor, comfortably clearing the 20 dB signal-to-noise gap that broadcast and podcast engineers commonly treat as the minimum for a clean, edit-friendly dialogue track.

Because the gap clears that threshold with margin, you leave the setup where it is instead of relocating to the bathroom, a common fallback for hotel-room recording. Had L90 come in above roughly -42 dBFS — inside 20 dB of that same speaking peak — the specific next step would have been to switch the HVAC off at the thermostat, drop the FFT size to 4096 for a faster-reacting read, and rerun the same 30-second Leq window before deciding whether the room needs treatment or a straight relocation.

Signal Diagnostics: Waveform, VU Meters, and Levels

A frequency view alone doesn't catch everything, which is why a proper waveform oscilloscope panel is worth checking alongside the spectrum before you trust a recording.

RMS, Peak Hold, and Clipping

VU meters and a peak hold readout give you a running sense of level even when you glance away from the screen for a second. Watch the raw waveform for flat-topped peaks — that's clipping, hard digital distortion from a signal driven past 0 dBFS, and no amount of EQ afterward fixes it. If you see it, the fix is at the source: pull down the input level, back the mic off-axis, or drop the preamp trim, not the analyzer settings.

Signal Chain and Proximity Effect Checks

The oscilloscope is also the fastest way to sanity-check your signal chain — DC offset, an unusually asymmetric waveform, or self-noise from a bad cable all show up here before they'd be obvious in the frequency view. Moving a cardioid mic closer to a sound source boosts low frequencies through proximity effect; watching the bass bands swell on the spectrum as you lean in is the quickest way to see that effect happening rather than just reading about it.

Spectral Centroid and Tone Brightness

The spectral centroid reading is the amplitude-weighted average frequency of everything currently playing — effectively a single number for "how bright does this sound." A centroid sitting up around 4–8 kHz means a bright, treble-forward signal; one down near 200–500 Hz means something warm or bass-heavy. Tracking it over a session is a fast way to notice a mix or a mic position drifting brighter or darker than your reference without re-listening to the whole thing.

Mono Compatibility and Stereo Analysis

If your setup captures two channels, stereo analysis splits the signal into Mid (identical content in both channels) and Side (the difference between them). A strong side signal means wide, spacious stereo; a side signal near zero means the source is effectively mono. Checking mono compatibility this way before a mix ships matters because summed playback on a phone speaker or a mono PA can cancel content that sounded fine in headphones.

Real-World Uses for This Frequency Analyser

A general-purpose frequency analyser earns its keep across more situations than most people expect on first glance.

Mixing and Mastering with a Spectrum Analyser

During mixing and mastering, an EQ reference view like this one helps you spot frequency clashes — a bass guitar and a kick drum fighting for the same octave, or a vocal and a lead guitar both crowding 2–4 kHz — well before they turn into a muddy final mix. Producers and sound engineers commonly keep this kind of frequency analyser open in a second window purely as a sanity check against reference tracks.

Room Modes and Speaker Measurement

Position a measurement mic at your listening spot, play a sweep or broadband noise through your monitors, and any room modes — the peaks and nulls created by parallel walls and room dimensions — will show up as narrow spikes and dips, usually below 300 Hz. This kind of acoustic measurement is exactly the first step professional acoustic engineering firms take before recommending bass traps or repositioning speakers.

Broadcast Audio QC and Live Sound Checks

Before a stream or a broadcast goes out, a quick pass with this frequency analyser catches sub-20 Hz rumble, 50/60 Hz hum, and harsh high-frequency rolloff that would otherwise only surface after broadcast audio QC flags it. For live sound engineers, the same view doubles as a fast way to catch a ringing monitor frequency before it turns into feedback in front of an audience.

Getting the Most from Your Free Frequency Analyser

This free frequency analyser is meant to be used often, not just once — a few habits make it far more useful as a recurring diagnostic tool rather than a one-time curiosity. Bookmark it as your go-to online frequency analyser for quick checks, and it turns into a lightweight frequency checker you reach for reflexively before hitting record.

If you let a measurement run over a longer session rather than glancing at an instant reading, three extra stats become useful: Leq is the energy-equivalent average level across the whole session, L10 is the level exceeded 10% of the time — effectively your loud peaks — and L90 is the level exceeded 90% of the time, the quiet background floor underneath everything else. The gap between L10 and L90 tells you how variable a space's noise really is, which is exactly the kind of noise measurement a 15-minute environmental survey depends on.

Comparing Sessions with Reference Traces

Because it's an online frequency analyser with nothing to install, you can return to the same tab days later and pick up exactly where you left off. Save a snapshot as one of your reference traces before you change anything — move the mic, swap a cable, adjust EQ — then capture a second trace afterward. Overlaying both on the live spectrum turns "did that help?" into something you can actually see rather than guess at by ear, the same way any careful frequency checker workflow should.

Smoothing, Gain, and Display Settings

Smoothing controls how much the display's motion is averaged between frames, calming a jittery reading at the cost of responsiveness to a fast transient. Gain boosts a quiet signal so faint detail doesn't get lost near the noise floor. Between a logarithmic scale, which matches how pitch perception actually works, and a linear scale, which spreads equal space across equal Hz, logarithmic is almost always the better default for music and speech, while linear suits engineering-style signal work.

Exporting CSV Data for Further Analysis

CSV exports let you pull the raw spectrum, octave-band levels, and any saved comparison snapshots into a spreadsheet or a stats package for a more rigorous write-up than a screenshot allows — useful for noise measurement reports, audio diagnostics logs, or simply archiving a before-and-after for a client. Combined with peak detection, note identification, and an SPL meter-style readout, the exported data gives you a documented record instead of a memory of what the spectrum looked like.

Whether you're chasing a hum, checking a room, or just curious what your voice actually looks like as a spectrum, this frequency analyser turns an abstract signal into something you can see, compare, and act on — no audio recording uploaded anywhere, no software to install, just your microphone and a browser tab. Used consistently across sessions, it becomes less of a novelty and more of a habit that catches problems earlier, whether you work in music production, podcasting, or everyday sound level checks around the house.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a sound spectrum?
A sound spectrum is a representation of the energy present at different frequencies in an audio signal. Instead of showing amplitude over time, it shows which frequencies are dominant — for example, a bass-heavy recording will show high energy in the 20–250 Hz range. Spectrum analysis helps diagnose microphone coloration, room acoustics, and signal noise.
What do the frequency bands (Bass, Mid, Treble) represent?
Bass covers 20–250 Hz (low-end rumble, kick drums, bass guitar), Mid covers 250 Hz–4 kHz (vocals, guitars, most musical content), and Treble covers 4–20 kHz (air, cymbals, high-frequency detail). A balanced microphone should capture energy across all three bands without excessive coloration in any one range.
What is A/C/Z frequency weighting?
Weighting curves adjust measured dB levels to account for how humans perceive loudness at different frequencies. A-weighting (dBA) mimics the human ear's reduced sensitivity at low and very high frequencies and is used for environmental noise measurements. C-weighting (dBC) is flatter and used for peak measurements. Z-weighting (dBFS) is completely flat with no adjustment applied.
What is the crest factor and why does it matter?
The crest factor is the difference between the peak level and the RMS (average) level of a signal, expressed in dB. A high crest factor means the signal has sharp transients relative to its average loudness. Pink noise has a crest factor around 12–15 dB, while a sine wave is about 3 dB. Knowing the crest factor helps set appropriate headroom when recording.
What is FFT size and how does it affect the analysis?
FFT (Fast Fourier Transform) size determines the frequency resolution of the spectrum analysis. A larger FFT size (e.g. 16384) provides finer frequency detail, allowing you to distinguish closely spaced tones, but requires more processing time. A smaller FFT size (e.g. 2048) responds faster to changes but shows coarser frequency resolution.
What is the difference between Pink Noise and White Noise for testing?
White noise has equal energy per frequency (flat spectrum), making it useful for testing system frequency response. Pink noise has equal energy per octave band, which sounds more balanced to human ears and is preferred for room acoustic measurements and speaker/microphone calibration. Use pink noise when measuring a room or microphone's natural response.
What is 1/3 octave resolution versus 1/1 octave?
Octave resolution determines how finely the frequency spectrum is divided into bands. 1/1 octave gives 10 broad bands across the audible range — useful for quick overviews. 1/3 octave gives 31 narrower bands, revealing more detail about where specific frequency problems exist. Room acoustic measurements and equalizer tuning typically use 1/3 octave analysis.
What does smoothing do to the spectrum display?
Smoothing averages the spectrum over time so the display is less jittery and easier to read. A smoothing value of 0 shows raw, unaveraged data that updates very quickly. Higher smoothing values (toward 0.95) produce a slow, stable trace that is easier to interpret but less responsive to sudden transient events. Use low smoothing to catch peaks, and high smoothing to assess steady-state levels.
