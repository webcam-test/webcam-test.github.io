A rear camera test puts your phone's or webcam's back-facing camera through a quick, no-download check: grant access once and you get a live preview plus an accurate resolution reading in return. Whether you're chasing down a black screen before a call or just confirming the camera your device opened is actually the one you meant to check, running this test first saves you from guessing at the cause. This guide covers what the check measures, why it sometimes comes back with a blank preview or a mirrored image, and how to read the numbers it hands you.

What a camera test actually checks

A browser-based camera test opens the standard interface built into Chrome, Edge, Firefox, or Safari, asks for permission, and starts a live preview from whichever camera it treats as default. It reads the camera resolution that camera negotiated and shows the number beside the preview, then lets you save a single still frame as a JPEG. Nothing here involves recording video or uploading anything - the feed and the saved frame stay on your device throughout. That's the real difference between this kind of tool and a full camera check: one resolution readout and one saved photo, not a recording session. The check phone torch and flash works entirely client-side, so you get an instant result without installing an app or creating an account.

It also checks the camera only. A clean, sharp preview at the expected resolution confirms the sensor and its permission path are both working - it says nothing about your microphone level or whether your display has a dead pixel. Pair it with a microphone test and a display test if you're running a full pre-call check. Some sites label this a webcam test or simply a camera check, and a handful even fold in a front camera test on the same page - switch cameras rather than switching pages.

How to run a rear camera test in your browser

The mechanics are the same on desktop and mobile, with one extra step on a phone: The check phone zoom performance works entirely client-side, so you get an instant result without installing an app or creating an account.

  • Open the page and click Start Camera. A permission prompt appears immediately rather than a blank preview.
  • Allow the prompt. A blocked permission produces an instant black frame instead of a live preview, so this step is the one most people miss.
  • Switch to the back camera if the front one opens first. Most phones default to the front-facing camera; tap the switch-camera control to move to the rear lens.
  • Read the number next to the preview. It appears as soon as the stream starts, showing the negotiated width and height.
  • Save the shot if you want a copy. The download button writes exactly what the sensor saw at that instant to a JPEG.

None of this needs an account or an install. The whole exchange happens between your device and its own camera driver, which is also why the check keeps working if your connection drops right after the page finishes loading.

Front camera vs. back camera: what changes when you switch

On a phone, the front and rear cameras are different pieces of hardware doing different jobs, and this test exposes that difference directly. The front one is built for calls and selfies, so phone makers usually accept a lower reading there in exchange for faster autofocus at close range. The rear one is built for photos, so it typically negotiates a noticeably higher number.

Which camera the switch control actually targets

The front/rear switch camera control only shows up on devices with more than one lens exposed to the browser - in practice, phones and a handful of two-camera laptops. On a single-camera desktop or an external USB webcam, there's nothing to switch to, so the control simply doesn't appear.

Android occasionally hides an extra rear lens - a wide-angle or macro lens on a triple-camera phone, for instance - from the browser entirely. If tapping switch camera does nothing on a multi-lens phone, that's usually why: the browser only ever exposes one rear-facing feed, not every physical lens behind it.

Reading the resolution number

The number the test reports is the actual size the browser was handed, not a sensor's marketing megapixel rating. A camera advertised as "12 MP" can still return a modest live-preview figure if another app already holds the high-quality mode, or if the browser negotiated a smaller frame for performance.

That figure is reported as width times height in pixels, and the total pixel count follows directly from those two numbers:

$$\text{total pixels} = \text{width} \times \text{height}$$

A back camera that reports 3840 x 2160 is delivering:

$$3840 \times 2160 = 8{,}294{,}400 \text{ pixels} \approx 8.3\text{MP}$$

while the front one on the same phone might settle for 1280 x 720:

$$1280 \times 720 = 921{,}600 \text{ pixels} \approx 0.9\text{MP}$$

Megapixels, in other words, are just that pixel total divided by a million: \(\text{MP} = \dfrac{\text{width} \times \text{height}}{1{,}000{,}000}\). A smaller figure after you switch cameras reflects the mode each one actually negotiated, not a drop in quality.

Aspect ratio shifts alongside that figure, and it's worth knowing the shorthand for it: divide width by height and reduce the fraction. A 3840 x 2160 reading reduces to 16:9 - \(\frac{3840}{2160} = \frac{16}{9}\) - the standard widescreen shape most back cameras default to for a live feed. A 1280 x 960 reading, which some older front cameras still negotiate, reduces to 4:3 - \(\frac{1280}{960} = \frac{4}{3}\) - a boxier frame that was standard before widescreen displays took over. Neither shape is a fault; it's just what the negotiated mode happens to use, and cropping a 4:3 photo down to 16:9 afterward always loses some of the original frame at the top and bottom.

Camera tests commonly settle into one of a handful of standard tiers, and recognizing them saves you from redoing this math every time: 640 x 480 (a legacy fallback most modern hardware skips past), 1280 x 720 (the usual front-camera tier), 1920 x 1080, and 3840 x 2160 (the higher end most modern back cameras reach in a browser). A camera test that returns anything below the 640 x 480 floor on a recent phone usually points at a stalled negotiation rather than a genuinely low-quality sensor - reload the page rather than assuming the hardware itself is at fault.

Troubleshooting a camera test that shows no signal

A no-signal result has a short list of usual causes, and checking them in this order gets you to the fix fastest.

Browser permission: the most common block

Click the camera icon in the address bar and confirm browser permission is set to Allow. On Chrome, that's under Site settings → Camera; if you clicked Block on an earlier visit, the prompt won't reappear on its own, and the page will sit there indefinitely until you fix the setting by hand. This is separate from any OS-level camera permission, covered next.

Chrome, Edge, Firefox, and Safari behave a little differently

Chrome and Edge remember a permission per site until you clear it. Firefox asks again more readily after certain privacy-setting changes. iOS Safari requires a user tap before it will read the camera at all - a page that auto-starts on load still sits blank on an iPhone until you interact with it once.

Secure connection: why plain HTTP won't work

Browsers expose the camera API only over https://; a page served over plain HTTP returns no stream at all, regardless of what permission you grant. This secure origin requirement is built into the browser itself, not into the test page, so there's nothing to configure beyond making sure the address bar actually starts with the secure prefix.

OS-level camera privacy settings by platform

Most operating systems have their own privacy switch sitting above the browser, so a camera can stay dark even after you click Allow in the browser itself.

Windows

Open Settings, then Privacy and security, then Camera, and confirm both overall access and desktop-app access are turned on. A single toggle left off here overrides any permission you've already granted.

macOS

Open System Settings, then Privacy and Security, then Camera, and make sure your browser is checked in the list. macOS blocks access per application, so granting it to one browser doesn't carry over to another you switch to later.

Linux

Confirm your account actually has access to /dev/video0 (or the equivalent device node). A permissions issue at this level looks identical to a browser-side block, which is worth checking even after the browser itself reports the camera as allowed.

Checking a used phone's camera before you pay

The seller's listing for a used Pixel 7a lists it at $187, "excellent condition, camera works fine." Before sending payment through the marketplace app, you ask to meet in person and pull the listing photo up next to the phone itself. The rear housing looks clean, no scratches over the lens, but a look isn't a test.

You open the browser on the phone, load the check, and tap Allow when the permission prompt appears. The preview comes up immediately - no black frame, no delay - and the resolution reads out as 3840 x 2160, right in line with what a working 64MP sensor should negotiate for a live feed once the browser caps it to a standard video mode rather than a full still capture. You point the phone at the seller's shop sign across the room and the text renders sharp, not smeared or doubled, which rules out a cracked internal lens element that a quick glance wouldn't catch.

Then you tap switch camera to bring up the front lens, and it settles at 1280 x 720 - lower, as expected, since it's built for calls rather than photos. Both numbers landing where they should, on the first try, with no permission fight and no held-open app from a prior owner's account still running in the background, is the actual signal you were waiting for. A resolution reading that matched the spec sheet but arrived only after three reloads and a factory-reset prompt would have meant a deeper hardware issue riding along with a lucky pass; here it didn't.

You hand over the $187, because the number that came back - 3840 x 2160 on the rear lens, sharp and immediate - is the one figure that separates "camera works fine" as a claim from "camera works fine" as something you actually confirmed before the money changed hands.

When another app is already holding the camera

A blank preview often means another program - Zoom, Teams, OBS, or the native camera app - already has the device open in exclusive mode. Quit the other app and reload the test; on a laptop this is the single most common cause of a preview that stays dark despite every setting looking correct. This shows up constantly on shared work laptops, where a conferencing app set to launch at startup keeps a background connection to the camera open for hours after the last meeting ended. Windows and macOS both surface a small camera-in-use indicator - a colored dot near the menu bar or system tray - specifically so you can spot this without opening every running app one at a time.

  • Check your taskbar or dock for a call app you forgot was still running.
  • On Windows, an indicator light that stays on after you close an app is a sign it hasn't fully released the device yet - give it a few seconds and reload.
  • A physical lens cover or a laptop's BIOS disable switch produces the exact same solid black frame, so rule those out too before assuming a software conflict.

When the preview comes back as a black frame

A solid black frame is a distinct failure from a blank preview - the stream is technically connecting, but nothing usable is coming through. The usual causes, in diagnostic order, are a physical shutter or lens cover, a driver conflict, a codec restriction in the browser, and a second app holding the device in exclusive mode. Work through them in that sequence rather than jumping straight to reinstalling anything, since the first two causes are far more common in practice.

Frame rate and permission prompts differ by device

A desktop browser typically reports a frame rate alongside the resolution; a mobile reading is accurate but limited to whatever mode the OS reports, without a separate frame-rate figure. Prompts also differ: desktop usually asks once per site per OS user, while mobile can ask again each new tab unless you've saved the choice.

Saving a photo from the test

The save action writes the raw, un-mirrored sensor frame the instant you press the button - not the flipped or filtered version you might be looking at on screen. That's why a saved photo containing text reads correctly even when the on-screen preview showed it backwards. It lands on your device as a JPEG, typically named with a word like "captured" followed by a timestamp, so a second shot never silently overwrites the first.

Editing the saved photo

Once you've got a frame, an Edit control on many camera-test pages hands that shot to an on-site photo editor already loaded, so you can crop or adjust it without downloading first. The file stays on your device throughout - nothing is uploaded to run the edit.

Many pages call this button Capture rather than Save, but the two labels do the same job: press it and the browser grabs whatever the sensor is showing at that exact instant, ready for the download step. Live preview filters, if the page offers any, are cosmetic - they change what you see while you frame the shot but never reach the file that capture step produces, which is why a black-and-white filter during the preview still yields a full-color JPEG once you download it. Adjust your pose or angle before you press capture rather than after; there's no undo once the frame is saved.

Flip vs. mirror: why your preview looks backwards

By default, most camera tests show a mirrored view - the same left-right swap you'd see in a bathroom mirror - because it's easier to frame against while you're looking at your own screen. A control toggles that mirroring for the live view, and unlike a color filter, it's carried through to whatever you save: turn it on before saving and the file comes out flipped to match what you saw on screen. Turn it off first if the shot needs to include readable text - a whiteboard, a document, a screen in the background - since a mirrored save will show that text backwards.

USB webcams and driver issues on desktop

A built-in laptop webcam and a plugged-in USB webcam both preview through the same browser interface, so neither needs a special mode. A few desktop-specific issues are still worth ruling out:

  • Unplug and reconnect the webcam, then update its driver - a stale one still attached to an app that already closed is a common cause of a preview that won't start.
  • When several cameras are connected at once, the browser picks a default on its own; there's no in-page switch control for a desktop setup the way there is for a phone's front and rear lenses.
  • A restart clears most driver conflicts that a simple unplug-and-reconnect doesn't.
  • Cable quality matters more than people expect: a webcam pushed through a long, low-quality cable can drop frames or negotiate a lower resolution than the same webcam gets on a short, well-shielded one, even though nothing about the camera itself changed.
  • If a webcam tests fine on one port but returns a grainy or stuttering preview on another, try a different cable before assuming the webcam itself is failing - a USB hub bridging older USB-A ports to USB-C peripherals is a common source of this kind of inconsistent behavior.

Testing your camera before a video call or interview

Running the check before a call catches a blocked permission or a camera still held by another app before the meeting starts, rather than in front of everyone once it has already begun. The live preview here is the same feed the browser hands to a video-call app, so if the picture is clear and the number reads as expected, you're genuinely ready.

Tip: run the test a few minutes before an interview, not the moment it starts - that gives you time to fix a permission block without anyone waiting on you.

A few things this check does not cover

A live preview and a resolution reading confirm the camera, the sensor, and the browser's camera permission path are all working end to end - but a handful of related questions sit just outside what a single camera resolution readout can answer. Whether your camera access settings carry over to a different browser on the same device is one of them: Android and iOS Safari both gate permission per browser, not system-wide, so allowing the camera in Chrome doesn't automatically allow it in Firefox or in a video call app that opens its own embedded browser view.

The camera API itself doesn't expose which permission prompt a given site triggered in the past, either - if you're troubleshooting on someone else's device, checking the browser's own site settings page directly is faster than guessing from the test result alone. And a photo editor bundled into the page can crop or adjust a saved JPEG, but it can't retroactively fix a low resolution reading; if the number was low because another app held the high-quality mode, editing the file afterward doesn't recover detail that was never there in the first place. A camera driver issue on desktop behaves the same way - reinstalling it after a bad shot doesn't repair that one file, only the next one you take.

A quick pre-check routine you can repeat every time

The same short sequence works whether you're checking a camera test result before a call, after switching browsers, or on a new device for the first time.

What to check every time

  • Confirm the URL starts with https before anything else - a plain HTTP page will never show a stream.
  • Grant camera access when prompted, and check the address-bar icon if the prompt never appears.
  • Switch to the back camera if your device defaults to the front one, and note the number that comes back.
  • Close any other app that might already hold the device - a call app left open in the background is the single most common blocker.
  • Check a saved photo isn't mirrored if the shot needs to include readable text.

Once those five checks come back clean, the camera, the permission path, and the negotiated resolution are all confirmed working - there's nothing else the test itself can tell you beyond that. None of it requires special hardware, an account, or a paid app: the same short sequence works identically on a five-year-old laptop and on a brand-new phone, and running through it takes less time than it took to read this far.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I start the rear camera test?
Select 'Rear Camera' from the Camera to Test option, then click the Start Test button. Your browser will ask for camera permission — tap Allow. The live preview from your back-facing lens will appear immediately, along with your detected resolution.
Why is my camera showing a black screen?
A black screen usually means camera permission was denied, another app is already using the camera, or the lens itself has a hardware fault. Try closing other apps that use the camera, refresh the page, and grant permission when prompted. If the black screen persists, the rear lens may need a physical inspection.
Is my camera footage recorded or uploaded anywhere?
No. The live preview runs entirely in your browser using the WebRTC/getUserMedia API. No video frames, images, or data are sent to any server. Everything stays on your device.
Can I test my rear camera on a smartphone or tablet?
Yes. This test works on Android and iOS devices in a modern browser such as Chrome, Safari, or Firefox. On mobile, selecting 'Rear Camera' activates the back-facing lens specifically, so you can verify it independently from the front camera.
What does the detected resolution mean?
The detected resolution shows the actual width × height (in pixels) that your browser is capturing from your rear camera. A higher resolution indicates more detail. If the resolution is much lower than your camera's advertised spec, your browser or device may be limiting the stream.
What camera problems does this test reveal?
This test can reveal a black or frozen frame (hardware failure), persistent blur (dirty or cracked lens, faulty autofocus), moving spots or smudges (debris on the sensor or lens), and failure to detect any camera at all (driver or permission issues). It does not replace a full hardware bench test for physical faults.
Why should I test my camera before a video call?
Checking your rear or front camera before an important video call ensures the lens is working, focused, and streaming at a usable resolution. It helps you catch permission errors or hardware problems in advance so you are not troubleshooting mid-call.
What is the difference between testing on desktop vs mobile?
On desktop, 'rear camera' typically maps to an external USB webcam if present, since laptops have only a front-facing camera. On smartphones and tablets, selecting 'Rear Camera' specifically activates the back lens. Mobile browsers support facingMode constraints that desktop browsers largely ignore.
