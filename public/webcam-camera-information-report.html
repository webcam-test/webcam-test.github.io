Asking what camera do I have, and what your web camera can actually do? Run the Camera Information Report and your browser will test your camera in seconds, then list its device name, real resolution, FPS and aspect ratio, plus the light and colour readings that decide how you look on video calls or when streaming. Nothing to install: the report reads the webcam and its microphone straight through the browser.

The report turns what your camera tells the browser into one table of specifications. Every row below is measured from the live stream, not copied from the box, which is why the webcam information here can differ from the seller's listing. Webcam Name and Built-in Microphone Detection

The webcam name is the device name the system registers, so it is the quickest way to confirm which camera the browser picked when an external webcam and a built-in one are both connected. The report also flags a built-in microphone or built-in speaker on the same webcam camera; treat that row as a hint, because some drivers report audio devices loosely.

Webcam Resolution, Megapixels and Aspect Ratio

The webcam resolution is the largest frame size the camera streams, and two other rows are derived from it:

$$\text{Megapixels} = \frac{\text{width} \times \text{height}}{1{,}000{,}000}$$

Take a 2K webcam reporting 2560 × 1440. That is 3,686,400 pixels, or 3.69 megapixels. Dividing both sides by their greatest common divisor (160) gives 16 × 9, so the aspect ratio is 16:9 and the video standard is QHD (1440p). Each frame carries four times the pixels of a 1280 × 720 HD camera, and a single megapixel more or less is easy to see in the comparison below.

  • VGA, 640 × 480: 0.31 MP, 4:3
  • 1080p, 1920 × 1080: 2.07 MP, 16:9
  • QHD, 2560 × 1440: 3.69 MP, 16:9
  • 4K UHD, 3840 × 2160: 8.29 MP, 16:9

Frame Rate and Video Standard

The same 2K webcam delivering 24 frames per second paints a new frame every 41.7 ms. Uncompressed, 3,686,400 pixels × 24 bits × 24 frames is about 2.12 Gbit/s, which is why every video conferencing app compresses the stream; the report's bitrate row estimates the bandwidth that compressed stream will use. The video standard row simply names the format the resolution matches.

Image Quality and Number of Colors

For image quality, the report samples a frame and counts the number of colors in it: more colours usually mean better optics and better light. Brightness, lightness and luminosity show exposure, while hue and saturation show tint and vividness. A frame with very few colours, or one stuck in black and white, points to poor light or a muted stream.

How to Test Your Webcam and Read the Results

To test your webcam, this free webcam test runs in four steps, and you can repeat it for each camera you own: The is my camera on works entirely client-side, so you get an instant result without installing an app or creating an account.

  1. Pick the camera from the device list if you have more than one, then click "Test my webcam".
  2. Click Allow when Chrome, Safari or Firefox asks to use the camera.
  3. Watch the webcam viewer: if you can see yourself, the video track is live.
  4. Wait for the table to fill, then take snapshots or switch to fullscreen to check the picture.

Run it again whenever you plug in a new camera; the webcam testing tool resets for each device you check your webcam with.

Camera Access and Browser Permissions

No report appears until the browser has camera access. If you clicked Block by mistake, reset the browser permissions:

  • Chrome and Edge: click the icon left of the address bar, set Camera to Allow, then reload.
  • Firefox: click the camera icon in the address bar and remove the blocked permission.
  • Safari: open Settings, choose Websites, then Camera, and set this site to Allow.

Chrome, Firefox and Safari only ask once per site: allow the camera here and you can revoke it later from the same settings.

How the Maximum Resolution Is Detected

Browsers don't announce a camera's limits directly. The tool lists your media devices, then requests progressively larger frame sizes and keeps the largest supported resolution the camera accepts. That size is the maximum resolution, which can sit below the box figure if the USB connection or the driver caps it.

Checking a Web Camera's Frame Rate Before an Evening Lesson

I teach maths online at 7:30 pm, and students keep telling me my whiteboard writing looks smeared. Before buying a new camera, I want to know whether the laptop's built-in 1080p camera is the problem. After three runs of the report, the answer is clear: the camera is fine, the room is too dark, and the darkness is halving the frame rate.

At 7:15 pm, with only the ceiling light on, I start the test and let the table fill. The webcam information shows 1920 × 1080, 2.07 MP and 16:9, so resolution isn't what's smearing the board. The Frame Rate row reads 14.9 fps: one new frame every 67.1 ms, roughly twice the 33.4 ms interval of the 29.97 fps NTSC rate most webcams are built around. A marker stroke drawn mid-frame simply blurs.

The cause is auto-exposure. A sensor can't deliver frames faster than it collects light, so the ceiling is:

$$\text{max frame rate} = \frac{1}{\text{exposure time}}$$

If the camera needs 1/15 s (66.7 ms) per frame in that light, 15 is its limit, which matches the 14.9 reading almost exactly. To rule out a busy computer, I close every other app and rerun, then switch on a desk lamp and rerun once more:

Checking a Web Camera's Frame Rate Before an Evening Lesson
SetupFrame rateTime per frame
Ceiling light only14.9 fps67.1 ms
Ceiling light, other apps closed14.8 fps67.6 ms
Desk lamp facing me29.8 fps33.6 ms

Closing apps changes nothing; the lamp doubles the frame rate. So I keep the laptop camera and move the lamp to my desk permanently, which costs nothing.

The number to watch here is the frame rate, not the megapixels. A slow reading looks exactly like a weak computer, and plenty of people upgrade a webcam that was only starved of light. Two caveats: run the report in the light you will actually teach in, because a noon test would have shown 30 fps and hidden the problem; and if the rate stays near 15 even under bright light, check the camera's driver software for a low-light mode, which deliberately trades frame rate for brightness, before you blame the hardware.

Why a Webcam Test Fails

When the viewer stays black, the cause is nearly always one of these, so work through them in order:

  • Camera blocked: you clicked Block instead of Allow; reset it as shown above.
  • Camera busy: Zoom, Teams or Google Meet is holding the camera, so close them and reload.
  • Disabled in system settings: check the camera switch in Windows or macOS privacy settings.
  • Drivers: on Windows, update them in Device Manager, or get them from the maker's site.
  • Connection: reseat the cable or try another USB port.
  • Hardware: if it fails on a second computer too, the camera is faulty.

Troubleshooting order matters: permission first, software next, hardware last, because each step rules out the cheapest fix before an expensive one.

Grayscale and Low Resolution Warnings

Even a working camera can trigger a warning. A low frame rate usually means an overloaded system or poor light, since many webcams slow the shutter in the dark. A low resolution warning means the camera, or a setting in the operating system, is capping the frame size. Grayscale output, a single-colour frame or a frozen image suggests a power-saving mode or another app muting the stream, while heavy noise levels point back to lighting.

Checking a Laptop Webcam on Windows and macOS

The report works on any laptop, desktop or smartphone with a modern browser, whether it runs Windows, ChromeOS or Linux. To cross-check a laptop webcam outside the browser:

  • On Windows, open the Camera app from the Start menu and take a test photo.
  • On macOS, open Photo Booth and record a short clip.
  • For a branded camera, use the maker's own settings app to adjust focus and exposure.

If the camera app works but the browser doesn't, the problem is browser permission, not the webcam.

Video Quality Before Video Calls

Run the report about ten minutes before a webinar or other virtual events, in the room you'll actually use. Check video quality under your real lighting conditions: image sharpness, color balance and a smooth picture matter more on screen than an extra megapixel. A quick video recording played back afterwards shows you what other people will see.

Privacy When You Check Your Webcam Online

An online webcam test should never need to upload your face. Here, every camera and microphone measurement runs locally in the browser and the stream stays in your device's memory; nothing is recorded or stored, and closing the tab discards it. Results are estimates that depend on the browser and driver, so if a number looks wrong, rerun the webcam test online in a second browser before trusting it. That also makes the cam test a fair way to compare two cameras side by side.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does the camera information report show?
It lists your webcam's device name, maximum resolution, megapixels, aspect ratio, video standard, frame rate, image mode, built-in microphone and colour and brightness readings, all measured from the live stream rather than taken from the box.
Why is my webcam's resolution lower than advertised?
The report shows the largest frame size the camera actually accepts through your browser. A USB port, hub, driver or system setting can cap it below the figure on the box.
Why is my frame rate low?
Most often the room is too dark: auto-exposure lengthens each frame, and frame rate can't exceed one divided by the exposure time. A busy computer is the other cause, so close other apps and retest.
Why can't the test find my camera?
Check that the browser has permission to use the camera, that no app such as Zoom or Teams is holding it, that it isn't disabled in Windows or macOS privacy settings, and that its cable and drivers are fine.
How do I allow camera access in my browser?
Click Allow when the browser asks. If you blocked it, use the icon beside the address bar in Chrome, Edge or Firefox, or Safari's Websites settings, to set Camera to Allow, then reload.
Is my video uploaded during the test?
No. The measurements run locally in your browser and the stream stays in your device's memory; closing the page discards it.
