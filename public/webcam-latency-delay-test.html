What This Actually Measures

Every trial measures your visual reaction time plus the camera's actual capture-to-detection delay combined — a browser can't separate the two. This test subtracts a typical average human visual reaction time (~250 ms, slightly slower than the ~190 ms typical for reacting to sound) from your average, and reports a range rather than a falsely precise single number.

Because this only needs the camera to see you reacting — not to be pointed at a screen — it works with a front-facing laptop webcam, a phone's front or rear camera, or an external webcam, with no special physical setup required.

A camera latency test puts a number on the moment between moving your hand and seeing the preview catch up. Point your USB camera or laptop webcam at the running timer on your screen, freeze a frame, and you get the hardware latency of the whole chain in milliseconds. It runs in the browser and shows whether a low frame rate or a setting is to blame.

How the Camera Latency Test Measures Glass-to-Glass Latency

This method measures glass-to-glass latency: the time between light (photons) hitting the lens glass and the matching image lighting up the glass of your monitor. The camera films an on-page clock, and the preview shows an older copy of it. You subtract the older timestamp from the newer one: Use the webcam frames per second test to check your hardware is actually working the way you expect, right from this page.

$$\text{Total latency} = t_{\text{live}} - t_{\text{reflected}}$$

The result is your total latency, the sum of every stage below. An open-source Python script uses the same idea, as does filming a clock beside an HDMI monitor.

Running the Optical Loopback Step by Step

  1. Grant camera permission when your browser asks; the page reads your feed through getUserMedia (WebRTC).
  2. Aim the lens at the running millisecond timer so the timer and its preview are both in shot.
  3. Press Capture Frame to freeze a snapshot.
  4. Read the live timer and the reflected timer inside the snapshot, then subtract.
  5. Repeat four times and average, since one reading can be off by a whole frame.

If the preview stays black, close other apps using the device and test your webcam again.

Where Webcam Lag Builds Up in the Video Pipeline

Webcam lag isn't one delay. It's a chain of small ones. Each hardware stage holds the frame for a few milliseconds, and the loopback measures the total for the whole pipeline. The order tells you which bottleneck to fix first. Use the side-by-side webcam comparison tool to check your hardware is actually working the way you expect, right from this page.

Rolling Shutter and Sensor Exposure

Most webcams use a CMOS chip with a rolling shutter, which reads rows from top to bottom. Scanning one frame takes a full frame period: 33.3 ms at 30 frames per second and 16.7 ms at 60 fps. In low-light rooms, auto-exposure makes each shot last longer than the frame period, adding tens of milliseconds before the image leaves the chip.

Image Signal Processor and Compression

Next, the image signal processor runs debayering to rebuild full colour. It also handles white balance, autofocus and noise reduction. Heavy denoising compares several frames held in firmware memory, so it waits for frames that haven't arrived. On cheaper models, the processor then applies MJPEG or H.264 compression, adding time to every frame.

USB 2.0 Bandwidth, Drivers and the Browser Compositor

USB 2.0 tops out at 480 Mbps. That's far below what uncompressed full-HD video at 60 frames needs, which is why these webcams compress. When the data arrives, the operating system's UVC driver unpacks it, the browser decodes it, and the compositor waits for the next vertical refresh (V-Sync) to show the frame. A busy compositor queues frames, adding latency.

To test one, aim it at an iPad clock and take one photo of that clock beside the receiving screen; the gap between the two clocks is the latency.

Measuring Glass-to-Glass Latency for an Online Piano Lesson

Take a piano teacher who teaches over Zoom with a plug-in webcam clamped above the keyboard. Her students keep saying her hands land after the notes. She needs to know two things: is the gap big enough to fix, and should she buy a capture card or just change settings? The answer: settings first. Her camera latency fell from 146.6 ms to 99.0 ms, and an 89 ms audio delay closed the rest.

Her studio is lit by one ceiling bulb, and the camera is on auto-exposure at 1080p. She runs seven loopback readings instead of five, because the first two differed by 24 ms: 143, 151, 127, 164, 138, 147 and 156 ms. That adds up to 1,026 ms, or an average of 146.6 ms. Her USB mic adds about 10 ms, so sound arrives roughly 137 ms before the picture. The EBU R37 broadcast guideline allows sound to lead picture by no more than 40 ms, so she's more than three times over.

Next she switches on a desk lamp, sets manual exposure at 1/60 s and runs seven more readings: 96, 104, 89, 111, 99, 93 and 101 ms.

Ceiling bulb, auto-exposure146.6 ms136.6 msOver by 96.6 ms

I checked both averages by adding up the readings by hand. The break-even point is simple to work out: without an offset, video latency would need to be 50 ms or less (40 ms plus the mic's 10 ms). That's capture-card territory, not webcam territory. So she routes the camera through OBS, sets an 89 ms sync offset on the mic, and repeats the clap test. The clap and the hand contact now land within one frame of each other.

One caveat that's easy to miss: a loopback reading includes your own display. Her 60 Hz monitor adds up to 16.7 ms that students never see, so the camera's real share is a little lower. If you set the offset straight from the loopback figure, you over-delay the audio slightly. That's harmless at this size, but it matters when you're tuning a sub-50 ms setup. The number I'd watch is the lighting-driven jump. Exposure alone costs her 47.6 ms, more than any cable or port change could have saved.

Audio-Video Synchronization and Video Lag

The number you measured matters most when sound and picture travel separately. In content creation, video lag shows up as lips that move after the words are heard.

Why Audio Leads Video by a Few Frames

A microphone buffer holds a tiny slice of samples, while every frame passes through the readout, the image processor, the USB link and the compositor described above. That leaves a capture delay gap of several frames between the two. In the worked example, video trails by 87.2 ms. If your audio path adds about 12 ms, sound reaches the recording roughly 75 ms early.

Fixing Lip-Sync with a Sync Offset

To measure the real lip-sync gap instead of estimating it:

  • Record a short clip with the camera and mic together, and clap once in frame.
  • Open it in any video editor that shows the audio waveform.
  • Find the frame where your hands meet and measure how far it is from the waveform peak.
  • Enter that value as a positive sync offset on the mic source in OBS or whatever broadcast software you use.

Check it again whenever you change camera settings before a livestream, because a new frame rate moves the gap.

How to Reduce Hardware Lag and Display Lag

Once you know your starting number, a few changes cut lag at the source. Retest after each one.

Lock Shutter Speed and Use Manual Exposure

Switch the camera's settings app to manual exposure, set a shutter speed of 1/60 s or faster, and add lighting so the image stays bright. In our example, locking the camera at 60 fps this way brought the five-reading average down from 87.2 ms to 70.4 ms. That 16.8 ms gain matches the 16.7 ms you save by cutting the frame period in half.

Plug Into a USB 3.0 Root Port

Front-panel ports and unpowered hubs share a host controller, so use a rear root port. If the camera supports USB 3.0, the extra bandwidth carries frames without compression. Don't ask a weak camera for 4K either. Native 1080p at a sensible resolution keeps the processor from compressing harder.

Match Frame Rate to Refresh Rate

A stream of 30 or 60 frames per second doesn't divide evenly into a 144 Hz display, so frame pacing becomes uneven and you see micro-stutters. If you match the camera's output to a multiple of the display's refresh rate, you keep frame jitter low and avoid dropped frames. Your display's refresh is part of every loopback reading too. It's the same wait that adds input lag in gaming and makes a reaction time score on a 60 Hz panel look slower than a reaction time taken on a fast display, and it adds up to 16.7 ms to your result.

Webcam Test Checklist Before Zoom, Teams or Discord

Run the video latency test before an important call and check:

  • The right camera is selected and the resolution matches your setting.
  • Your average sits inside your camera type's range above.
  • Close any other app that may be using the camera, then run the webcam test again.
  • Run your mic check too, so both audio and picture work for video conferencing.

Browser Privacy and Local Processing

The test runs entirely in your browser. Frames stay in local memory and nothing is uploaded, which matters for privacy. If the page stays black after you allow access, check your operating system's camera privacy settings, which override the browser prompt.

Frequently Asked Questions

How does a camera latency test measure webcam lag?
You aim the webcam at a running millisecond timer so the timer and its own preview appear in the same frame. Capturing a snapshot freezes both values, and the live timer minus the reflected timer is your glass-to-glass latency.
Why can't the browser read the webcam's delay automatically?
getUserMedia hands the page finished frames only. Sensor exposure, debayering and USB buffer timestamps are not exposed to web pages, so a physical loopback photo is the reliable way to measure the total delay.
How many snapshots should I take?
At least three, ideally five or more. The timer only updates on a screen refresh and the camera samples once per frame, so single readings can differ by a full frame. The average is the number to trust.
Why does my webcam video lag behind my microphone audio?
Video frames pass through exposure, image processing, compression and the display compositor, which often adds 50-150 ms. Microphone buffers are tiny, so sound arrives first unless you add a sync offset to the mic in your streaming or call software.
What latency is normal for a webcam?
In a well-lit room, built-in laptop webcams usually measure roughly 80-200 ms, plug-in MJPEG webcams 70-130 ms, and cameras on an HDMI capture card 30-60 ms. Network IP cameras can run much higher.
Is my camera feed uploaded anywhere?
No. The preview, timer and snapshots stay in your browser's local memory. Nothing is recorded or sent to a server.
How can I lower my camera latency?
Add light and switch to manual exposure so the shutter stays short, connect the webcam directly to a rear USB port, choose 1080p instead of 4K on weaker cameras, and run the test again after each change.
