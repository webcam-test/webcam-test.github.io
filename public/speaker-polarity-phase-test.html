Speaker Polarity Test: Free Online Phase Check Tool

Reversed speaker wiring rarely sounds obviously broken, but the Speaker Polarity and Phase Test makes an out-of-phase pair unmistakable. Click Play In-Phase (Correct Wiring) to hear a centered, bass-solid baseline, then Play Out-of-Phase (Reversed Wiring) to hear the diffuse, bass-light sound two speakers make when they're working against each other — you'll need two real stereo speakers, not headphones, for the phase cancellation to actually happen in the air between them. What You're Listening For

In-phase (correct): the sound feels centred between your speakers with solid, present bass — a clear, focused image.

Out-of-phase (reversed): the sound feels diffuse and hard to locate, thinner overall, with noticeably weaker bass — the two speakers are partially cancelling each other out.

What Reversed Wiring Means

If your speakers sound like the "out-of-phase" example even during normal listening, one speaker's positive and negative wire connections are likely swapped relative to the other — common after DIY speaker wiring or a loose terminal. Swapping the two wires on just one speaker (not both) usually fixes it.

Run a speaker polarity test before you blame a receiver, a streaming app, or your ears for thin-sounding music — the real culprit is often one reversed speaker wire. When two speakers are wired correctly, their cones push air in the same direction and reinforce each other for deeper, more focused bass; when one is reversed, they fight each other and the sound collapses into something wide, hollow, and bass-light.

What a Speaker Polarity Test Actually Checks

A polarity test plays matched "in phase" and "out of phase" audio signals and asks you to listen for the difference. It doesn't measure voltage or continuity — it measures how your speakers, as a pair, are actually reproducing sound in your room. If your amplifier or receiver is fine and each speaker works individually, but stereo playback still sounds off, this test isolates whether wiring polarity between the two speakers is the problem. Nothing you do in the subwoofer test ever leaves your device — the whole test runs locally in your browser tab.

Absolute Polarity vs. Relative Polarity

Absolute polarity refers to whether a single speaker's cone moves outward or inward on a positive-going signal — something your ears genuinely cannot detect on one speaker alone. What you can hear is relative polarity: whether the two speakers in a stereo pair agree with each other. As long as the positive (+) and negative (−) terminals are wired the same way on both speakers, absolute polarity doesn't matter for how the pair sounds.

Running a Stereo Polarity Test at Home

This kind of stereo polarity test takes less than a minute per pair. Start at a low volume — 10–20% is plenty for a polarity check — and sit centered between your two speakers, at roughly the same distance from each. Centering matters because polarity problems are easiest to hear directly on the centerline, where a correctly wired pair should image a voice or instrument as a single, tightly focused point rather than a smeared, directionless blur. Nothing you do in the check stereo channel balance ever leaves your device — the whole test runs locally in your browser tab.

Keep the volume low and your head still while you compare clips — moving even a foot off-center can make a properly wired pair sound momentarily hollow, which is the opposite of what you want to judge polarity by.

In Phase vs. Out of Phase: What You're Listening For

Play the "in phase" clip first. It should sound centered, punchy, and full of low-end weight. Then play the "out of phase" version of the same clip: correctly wired speakers will make it sound wide, thin, and oddly directionless, with the bass seeming to drop out. If your speakers already sound this way on the "in phase" clip, one of them is wired backward.

The Physics Behind In Phase and Out of Phase Cancellation

The effect comes down to simple wave addition. When both speakers push air outward at the same instant, the pressure waves they create add together at the listening position. When one speaker is reversed, it pushes in while the other pushes out, and the waves subtract instead:

$$A_{combined} = A_1 + A_2 \quad \text{(in phase)}$$

$$A_{combined} = A_1 - A_2 \quad \text{(out of phase)}$$

Here \(A_1\) and \(A_2\) are the pressure contributions from each speaker at your ears. Bass frequencies suffer the most from this cancellation because their wavelengths are long relative to typical speaker spacing, so the two signals stay almost perfectly opposed across the whole room. A few test signals make this easy to hear:

  • A low-frequency rumble — in phase it should feel physically full; out of phase it thins out noticeably
  • A steady mid-bass tone — in phase it plays audibly louder than the out-of-phase version at the same volume setting
  • A short guitar recording or spoken phrase — in phase it sits solidly in the center; out of phase it sounds like it's coming from inside your head rather than in front of you

Left/Right Stereo Test and Polarity Audio Test Basics

A full polarity audio test usually sits alongside a basic left/right channel check, and it's worth running both together, since a wiring mistake that swaps channels can also mask or mimic a polarity problem.

Checking Left and Right Channels First

Play a tone routed only to the left channel, then only to the right. Sound should come from the matching speaker every time; if it comes from the wrong side or from both, fix that channel routing before you even attempt the polarity comparison — a channel-swap issue will confuse the phase test results.

Troubleshooting a Silent Browser Test

If the test page plays nothing at all, the problem is almost always the browser audio path rather than your speakers. Work through this list before assuming a hardware fault:

  • Check that the browser tab and system volume mixer aren't set to mute for that specific tab or app
  • Confirm the correct output device is selected — a laptop can silently default to HDMI or a disconnected headset instead of your speakers
  • Try a different set of test tones or a short tone generator clip to rule out a stuck audio buffer
  • Test with headphones or an earbud plugged in; if those work, the fault is downstream in your speaker connection, not the browser

Frequency Response and the Frequency Sweep Test

Polarity issues often get blamed on frequency response problems, so it helps to test both. A frequency sweep test plays a continuous tone rising from around 20Hz up to 20kHz, letting you hear exactly where your speakers, your subwoofer, and your room start to struggle.

  • Deep bass response, where a subwoofer or a large driver carries the signal
  • Midrange clarity, where vocals and most instruments live
  • Treble extension, where a tweeter has to reproduce fast, high-frequency detail

Reading a Frequency Sweep Test for Polarity Clues

Listen for spots where the tone suddenly gets quieter, buzzes, or seems to vanish — that can point to a crossover interaction or resonance in the cabinet, not necessarily polarity. But if the entire low end sounds consistently weak across the whole sweep, and the "out of phase" clip sounds similar, reversed wiring is the more likely cause. Rattling or buzzing at specific notes, by contrast, usually means a loose panel or grille rather than a wiring fault.

Sine Wave Generator and Noise Generator Checks

A sine wave generator lets you isolate a single frequency instead of a sweep — 440Hz (the standard tuning-fork pitch) or 1kHz are common starting points for a clean, repeatable reference tone. A noise generator is a different kind of check entirely: white noise carries equal energy at every frequency and sounds like sharp static, while pink noise carries equal energy per octave and sounds noticeably deeper and smoother. Audio engineers reach for pink noise specifically because its balance matches how the human ear perceives loudness across the spectrum, which makes it a good complement to a polarity test rather than a replacement for one.

Stereo Imaging Test and Dynamic Range Test Context

A polarity problem shows up most clearly in a stereo imaging test, since imaging depends entirely on both speakers agreeing on timing and direction. A dynamic range test, by comparison, mostly reveals background noise and headroom limits in your equipment — it's a useful companion check, but it won't reveal a reversed wire the way an imaging or polarity test will.

Why Stereo Imaging Suffers From Reversed Polarity

Good stereo imaging and sound localization depend on your brain comparing tiny timing and level differences between your two ears. When speakers are out of phase, those cues become contradictory instead of consistent, and the stereophonic illusion collapses — instead of a stable, three-dimensional soundstage, you get a smeared, hard-to-place wash of sound. Distortion and harmonic distortion are separate problems entirely, caused by an overdriven amplifier or a stressed driver rather than wiring, so don't confuse a distorted, crackling sound with the smoother "wrongness" of a phase issue.

How to Fix Reversed Speaker Wiring

Once a polarity test confirms the fault, the fix is almost always mechanical, not electronic: flip the two speaker wires on one speaker only, never both, since flipping both leaves you back where you started. Trace each cable from your amplifier's positive and negative terminals to the matching terminal on the speaker, checking for a swapped connection, a mislabeled banana plug, or bare wire that's been reversed at either end.

Checking Positive and Negative Terminals

Every speaker terminal is marked positive or negative, and the wire connected to it should be marked or colored to match — most commonly a red or striped conductor for positive, plain black for negative. Confirm the same color lands on the same polarity terminal at the amplifier end and the speaker end, on both speakers, and re-check the listening position afterward rather than assuming the fix worked.

Tracing a Hollow Mix Back to One Swapped Wire

A pair of new bookshelf speakers goes up on stands 2.1 meters apart, angled in toward a couch set 1.7 meters back — a standard near-field triangle. The first track played is a familiar acoustic mix with a centered vocal and a steady kick drum, one used dozens of times before on the old pair. Something is off immediately: the vocal doesn't sit in the middle, it smears across the whole width of the room, and the kick barely registers at a volume where it used to thump.

Rather than guessing, the next step is the polarity test. The "in phase" clip plays first, and it sounds exactly like the mix problem — wide, diffuse, and thin below 100Hz. That's the tell: a correctly wired pair should make the "in phase" clip sound punchy and centered, with the "out of phase" clip being the one that sounds hollow. Here, both clips sound almost identical, which points straight at one reversed speaker rather than a source or room issue.

Tracing the cable from the right speaker back to the amplifier's terminals turns up the cause: the last owner had crimped a replacement banana plug onto the wire, and the plug's positive lead is seated in the negative post. The left speaker's wiring is untouched and correct. The fix takes under a minute — desolder and re-crimp the plug the right way around, positive to positive, negative to negative — and the polarity test is re-run immediately rather than trusted on sight.

This time the difference is unmistakable: the "in phase" clip locks the vocal dead center and the kick drum's low end fills the room the way it should, while the "out of phase" clip now sounds noticeably wide and weak by comparison — the expected pattern, reversed from before. With the wiring confirmed, the original acoustic mix comes back correctly centered, kick drum restored, and the room is ready for actual listening instead of more troubleshooting.

Common Signs Your Speakers Are Out of Phase

Beyond the formal in-phase/out-of-phase comparison, a handful of everyday symptoms point to a reversed speaker before you even run a dedicated test: bass that sounds thin no matter how you adjust the subwoofer level, a center vocal that never quite locks into a focused sound, or a mix that sounds noticeably wider and airier than it should, at the expense of any real low end. None of these alone is proof, but together with a failed polarity check, they confirm the diagnosis.

Testing Headphones and Earbuds for Reversed Wiring

Polarity checks aren't limited to floor-standing loudspeakers. Headphones and a single earbud pair can also be wired or paired incorrectly, especially with cheaper or mismatched replacement cables, and the same in-phase/out-of-phase listening test applies. This matters even for hearing-related checks: if you're also running a separate hearing test or screening for hearing loss, a polarity fault in your headphones can distort the results by changing how directional and bass cues actually reach your ears, and can quietly reduce stereo separation you'd otherwise notice right away.

When to Re-Test After New Speakers or Amplifier Wiring

Run a fresh polarity check any time you install new speakers, replace speaker cable, or rewire an amplifier — even a single loose or re-terminated connection is enough to flip one channel's polarity. It only takes a minute, and catching a reversed wire immediately is far easier than diagnosing "flat-sounding" audio weeks later, once you've already stopped suspecting the wiring.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I know if my speakers are out of phase?
The clearest sign of out-of-phase speakers is a significant loss of bass when both speakers play together compared to one speaker alone. The stereo image also feels vague or unfocused — sounds may appear to come from all around rather than from a defined center. Running an in-phase vs out-of-phase test tone at 80–120 Hz is the most reliable way to confirm this.
Why does the sound seem to disappear in 'Out of Phase' mode?
When two speakers are out of phase, one cone pushes air forward while the other pulls it back simultaneously. At your listening position — centered between the speakers — these equal and opposite pressure waves cancel each other out. This is most noticeable at lower frequencies where wavelengths are long enough to interfere across typical room distances.
What frequency should I use to test speaker polarity?
Use frequencies between 80 Hz and 200 Hz for polarity testing. At these frequencies, phase cancellation is most audible as bass reduction. Frequencies below 40 Hz are hard to hear without a subwoofer, and frequencies above 500 Hz have wavelengths short enough that room reflections complicate the result.
Can I use this test on headphones?
Yes — headphones can be tested for left/right channel identification and basic polarity checks. However, out-of-phase cancellation is most dramatic with speakers in a shared acoustic space. With headphones, out-of-phase content sounds 'wide' or 'inside your head' rather than disappearing, since each driver acts independently.
What should I hear during an in-phase stereo test?
During a properly in-phase stereo test, you should hear a solid, centered bass tone with good weight and presence. The sound should seem to originate from directly between your two speakers. If the bass sounds thin or hollow, or the image shifts to the sides, your speakers may be wired out of phase.
What is the difference between absolute polarity and relative phase?
Absolute polarity refers to whether a single speaker cone moves out or in response to a positive voltage signal. Relative phase describes the timing and polarity relationship between two or more speakers. While your ears cannot detect absolute polarity on a single speaker, relative phase between speakers is very audible — especially in the bass frequencies.
How do I fix out-of-phase speakers?
If your speakers are out of phase, simply swap the positive (+) and negative (–) speaker wire connections on ONE speaker (not both). This reverses that speaker's polarity and brings both speakers back into phase. Be sure to identify which terminal is positive (usually marked with a red or '+' symbol) before swapping.
Does successful phase testing mean my speakers are fully working correctly?
A phase test confirms that your speakers are wired with matching polarity, which is critical for bass response and stereo imaging. However, it does not test frequency response, distortion levels, driver damage, or crossover issues. Use the frequency sweep and individual channel tests alongside phase testing for a more complete speaker health check.
