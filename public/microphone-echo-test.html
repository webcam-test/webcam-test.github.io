What This Reveals

Hearing your own voice delayed is exactly what call participants experience — it makes room echo, a hollow/distant mic sound, or background noise far more obvious than listening to yourself normally, since we're used to hearing our own voice through bone conduction, not delayed through a speaker.

Reducing Echo

Soft furnishings (curtains, rugs, a couch) absorb reflections that hard-walled rooms bounce around. Moving your microphone closer to your mouth reduces how much room reflection it picks up relative to your direct voice, and pointing it away from bare walls or windows helps too.

How to Run a Mic Echo Test in Your Browser

Running a mic echo test takes less time than joining a video call. This free online echo test runs entirely in your browser — no plug-in, no download, no account — which makes it the fastest online echo test you can run before video calls, a recording session, or a livestream, anywhere your microphone needs to sound right. Some people call it a feedback loop test, an echo detector, or simply a feedback detector; whatever the name, run the echo test online again anytime you change rooms or switch headsets. Allow Mic Permission

Click the start button and grant mic permission when your browser prompts you. If nothing happens, check the address bar for a blocked-microphone icon — some browsers silently deny access until you approve the site manually in settings.

Speak Into Your Mic

Once access is granted, speak into your mic at your normal conversational volume. Say a few full sentences rather than a single word — a short "hello" won't give the analyzer enough signal to judge feedback risk or reverb accurately.

Watch the Live Echo Diagnostics

As you talk, a live oscilloscope traces your waveform in real time, and a frequency spectrum display breaks the signal into bass, midrange, and treble bands. An echo correlation plot compares your current input against a slightly delayed copy of itself — a flat line means nothing is repeating, while rising spikes flag an echo loop or open speaker feedback path. What Is Microphone Echo, and Why Does It Happen?

Microphone echo happens when your voice travels out through someone else's speakers, gets picked up again by their microphone, and comes back to you a moment later. It's a routing problem, not a hardware fault, which is why it can appear on a brand-new headset just as easily as an old laptop mic. Use the microphone input level meter to check your hardware is actually working the way you expect, right from this page.

What Triggers the Squeal?

This happens when a microphone continuously re-captures audio from its own speakers, amplifying the signal each pass until it turns into a rising squeal or howl. Any speaker feedback path — a laptop speaker sitting too close to an open mic, or a phone on speakerphone next to a headset — can trigger one.

Room Reverb vs. True Echo

Room reverb is different from a digital echo: it's caused by sound bouncing off hard surfaces — bare walls, desks, windows — and re-entering the microphone as a smeared blur rather than a distinct repeat. An acoustic reflection off one wall arrives a few milliseconds after another sound reflection off the ceiling, and together they stretch out the reverb tail that makes speech in an empty room sound distant and hollow.

Reading Your Signal Clarity Score

This score compares how clean your voice sounds against how much echo, reverb, or noise is layered on top of it. A high score means listeners hear you, not your room, and it's the single fastest number to check after making any of the fixes below.

Understanding Noise Floor

Your noise floor is whatever the mic picks up when nobody is speaking — fan hum, traffic, a humming monitor. Keeping that reading low gives cancellation software a cleaner signal to work with, which is part of why a quiet room fixes more audio problems than people expect, and why professional studios spend so much on soundproofing in the first place.

How to Fix Echo and Audio Feedback During a Microphone Test

Once a microphone test confirms echo or audio feedback is present, these fixes restore your sound quality and resolve the overwhelming majority of cases, roughly in order of effectiveness.

Turning On Echo Cancellation

Echo cancellation is built into almost every modern conferencing app and browser. Look for that feature in your audio settings and make sure it's switched on rather than left at its default — some apps quietly disable it when a "studio quality" or "original sound" mode is selected.

Acoustic Echo Cancellation in Zoom, Teams, and Meet

Turning on full acoustic echo cancellation only takes a minute per app. In Zoom, go to Settings → Audio → Suppress background noise and set it to High. In Microsoft Teams, open Settings → Devices → Noise suppression. In Google Meet, click the gear icon → Audio → Noise cancellation. Discord users can toggle it directly under Voice & Video. Confirming your own setup is clean with this test first tells you whether an echo on a call is coming from your end or theirs.

Filtering Background Noise

Background noise and echo often get blamed on each other. Rule out the culprits:

  • Close other apps that might still have the microphone open in the background
  • Move away from fans, air conditioning units, or open windows before you test again
  • Mute your microphone whenever you're not speaking during group calls
  • Close doors between you and noisy hallways, kitchens, or shared workspaces

When Noise Suppression Helps (and When It Hurts)

It filters out steady background hiss, but pushed too aggressively it can also chew off the tail end of your words, making you sound clipped. If your voice sounds robotic or cut-off after enabling it, dial the setting back a level and re-run the test.

Lowering Speaker Volume and Adding Soft Furnishings

Two low-effort fixes handle most remaining cases: reduce your speaker volume to 50–70% so audio can't travel far enough to loop back into the mic, and add soft furnishings — a rug, curtains, a couch — or dedicated acoustic panels to absorb reflections instead of letting them bounce back into an open microphone.

Catching Echo Before a Client Call

Priya has a client call in forty minutes, video on, from a rented apartment with tile floors and almost no furniture. Her last call ended with the client asking her to repeat herself twice, and she suspects the room rather than her connection. She opens the test, speaks for about fifteen seconds — her usual opening line for a project update — and watches the Live Echo Diagnostics panel settle: Echo Level reads clear, but Reverb Tail comes back elevated, and the oscilloscope shows her waveform trailing into a soft smear after each sentence instead of cutting off cleanly.

A Reverb Tail past roughly 300 milliseconds is close to the threshold broadcast engineers use to judge whether a reflected sound reads as a separate echo rather than part of the original voice. Her laptop's built-in mic, sitting flat on the desk about fourteen inches from her mouth, is picking up nearly as much sound reflected off the bare walls as it is direct voice.

She swaps in the small USB condenser mic she packed for exactly this kind of trip, positions it four inches from her mouth and angled slightly off-axis, and re-runs the test. Reverb Tail drops back into the panel's healthy range, and the Signal Clarity reading moves up with it. She pulls a folded blanket off the bed and drapes it over the chair behind the mic — a rough stand-in for acoustic panels she doesn't have on hand — and the reading holds steady on a third pass. With eight minutes left before the call, she joins Google Meet confident that what the client hears will match what she's hearing, not an echoey approximation of it.

Test Your Mic Setup Before Every Call

Beyond the immediate fix, a few setup choices determine whether echo becomes a recurring problem or a one-time glitch. It's worth the two minutes it takes to test your microphone setup once, properly, rather than troubleshooting it fresh before every important call.

Mic Positioning and Room Acoustics

Good mic positioning matters as much as any software setting: keep 6–12 inches of separation between your mouth and the capsule, and point the microphone away from your speakers rather than toward them. Room acoustics matter just as much — a carpeted room with furniture absorbs far more reflected sound than a bare office with hard floors and glass.

Choosing a Directional Microphone

A directional microphone rejects far more speaker bleed than a built-in laptop mic does. A cardioid microphone — the most common polar pattern for single-speaker use — picks up sound mainly from the front and rejects noise from the sides and rear. An omnidirectional mic, by contrast, captures sound equally from every direction, which is useful for a room of people but far more prone to echo and feedback. If your test keeps flagging feedback risk no matter how you adjust software settings, switching microphone types is often the fix that finally sticks.

Checking Sample Rate, Audio Channels, and Latency

Your microphone's sample rate, number of audio channels, and latency all show up in this test's device details. Low values here matter most for live streaming and gaming, where any delay between speaking and hearing yourself feels disorienting and can itself be mistaken for echo.

Build Quality: USB Mics, Pop Filters, and Impedance

A budget USB microphone with a built-in pop filter and shock mount — plus a foam windscreen — cuts down on plosives, handling noise, and wind rumble that can otherwise be mistaken for reverb or line noise on a test. Two specs to compare on the box: low self-noise (below 20dBA) and a favorable signal-to-noise ratio for clean, quiet recordings; the microphone's rated impedance and max SPL mainly matter if you're plugging into a mixer or recording something very loud.

Hearing Test: Check Your Ears as Well as Your Mic

Sometimes what sounds like echo isn't your microphone at all — it's your own hearing, or your speakers' limits. A quick hearing test rules that variable out.

Testing Your Hearing Range

A set of pure sine wave tones, played from low to high, reveals your personal hearing range. Most adults under 30 can hear up to 18–20kHz; that ceiling drops with age, and echo or heavy reverb makes tones sound doubled or sustained, which is itself a clue that your room needs acoustic treatment. Run this check once and note the result somewhere, since it's a useful baseline to compare against if your hearing or your equipment ever seems to change later on.

Checking Your Speakers' Frequency Response

The same tones expose your speakers' or headphones' frequency response. A tone you genuinely can't hear may simply be beyond what your hardware can reproduce, not a sign of hearing loss — worth knowing before you blame your ears for a hardware limit. Cheap laptop speakers often roll off well before 16kHz, which is one reason two people on the same call can disagree about whether an echo is even audible.

Troubleshooting: When the Mic Test Won't Detect Echo

Whether you're prepping for an interview, a podcasting session, a streaming setup, a webinar, or any other virtual events work, a clean result here should carry straight into video conferencing apps. If the mic test won't pick up your voice at all, work through these checks before assuming your hardware has failed.

No Media Devices Detected

If the page reports no media devices found, open your operating system's device settings and confirm a microphone is actually listed as connected — a loose USB cable or an unplugged headset shows up this way more often than a real fault.

Browser or Microphone Permission Denied

A denied prompt is the single most common cause of a failed test. Check browser permission in your site settings, and separately confirm microphone permission is enabled in your operating system's privacy settings — browsers and operating systems each maintain their own independent block list, so clearing one without the other still leaves you stuck.

Fixing Driver Issues in Device Manager

Persistent driver issues are worth ruling out next. Open Device Manager on Windows (or Audio MIDI Setup on a Mac), locate your microphone under sound devices, and update the audio driver — or uninstall and reinstall it if an update isn't offered. Restart your computer afterward before testing again; a driver update frequently doesn't take effect until every audio application, including your browser, has been fully closed and reopened.

Still Says Microphone Not Working?

If your microphone is still not working after all of the above, a few remaining checks resolve the rest:

  1. Confirm no other app — video call software, recording software, or a second browser tab — has locked the microphone exclusively
  2. Watch for visual feedback: if the input level meter never moves at all even while you speak, the wrong input device is likely still selected in system settings
  3. Confirm the audio track itself isn't muted or disabled at the hardware level, using a physical mute switch or button if your microphone has one

Once you can troubleshoot microphone issues this systematically, re-running this test takes ten seconds — and it's worth doing again anytime you change rooms, switch headsets, or install a system update, since any one of those can quietly reset your microphone settings back to defaults.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is microphone echo?
Microphone echo occurs when your audio output (from speakers or headphones) is picked up by your microphone and re-transmitted, causing listeners to hear a delayed repeat of their own voice or yours. It's most common with open-back headphones, external speakers, or high microphone gain settings.
Why do I need a mic echo test?
Running an echo test helps you identify whether your microphone setup is causing echo, feedback loops, or clarity issues before important calls, recordings, or streams. Catching these problems early saves you from embarrassing audio quality during meetings or content creation.
How does this echo test work?
This tool simulates an echo diagnostic based on your microphone type, device, gain settings, echo delay, feedback level, and environment. It scores your configuration across key audio quality factors — echo level, feedback risk, clarity, and reverb tail — then gives you an overall rating and actionable recommendations.
How do I fix microphone echo?
The most effective fixes include enabling echo cancellation in your OS audio settings or communication app, reducing microphone gain, switching from open-back to closed-back headphones, using a directional (cardioid) microphone, and moving to a quieter, acoustically treated room. USB and XLR microphones typically have better built-in echo rejection than integrated mics.
Why doesn't my microphone work properly?
Common causes include browser permission being blocked (you must allow microphone access), another application monopolizing the mic (e.g., Zoom or Discord), incorrect input device selected in your OS settings, outdated drivers, or a hardware fault. Try closing other apps, checking privacy settings, and reconnecting your mic.
What is microphone feedback?
Feedback is the high-pitched squeal or howl that occurs when a microphone picks up its own amplified output in a loop. It's caused by speakers being too close to the mic or gain being set too high. Lowering gain, using headphones instead of speakers, and enabling noise suppression all help reduce feedback.
Does microphone type affect echo?
Yes. Integrated (built-in) microphones are most prone to echo because they're physically close to the device's speakers. USB and XLR microphones, especially directional ones, have better isolation and are significantly less susceptible to picking up speaker output. Wireless mics can introduce additional latency that increases perceived echo.
Is my audio uploaded or stored when I run this test?
No. This echo test tool runs entirely in your browser. No audio is recorded, uploaded, or stored on any server. All calculations are performed locally based on the settings you enter.
