What This Reveals

Hearing your own voice delayed is exactly what call participants experience — it makes room echo, a hollow/distant mic sound, or background noise far more obvious than listening to yourself normally, since we're used to hearing our own voice through bone conduction, not delayed through a speaker.

Reducing Echo

Soft furnishings (curtains, rugs, a couch) absorb reflections that hard-walled rooms bounce around. Moving your microphone closer to your mouth reduces how much room reflection it picks up relative to your direct voice, and pointing it away from bare walls or windows helps too.

Echo Correlation Visualizer — Reading the Real-Time Waveform

The echo correlation visualizer is the centrepiece of this test tool. It plots the correlation between your outgoing speech signal and any delayed copy of that signal returning to your mic, making it possible to detect echo instantly. A high correlation spike appearing after a short delay (typically 20–200 ms) indicates a signal loop, while a broader, smeared correlation pattern points to room reverb and spatial reflection from hard surfaces. A clean mic working properly will show a sharp single peak at zero lag and minimal energy elsewhere. This visualizer gives you an at-a-glance answer: is your signal clean, or does it carry a ghost?

What This Microphone Echo Test Detects — Fixing Echo, Feedback, and Interference Problems

A mic test is not just a pass/fail check. Understanding what the tool has detected empowers you to make real improvements to your signal quality for broadcasting, content creation, music sessions, interviews, and digital interaction of every kind. Here is a breakdown of the four core problems this microphone echo test identifies, and the steps to fix each one. Signal Feedback Loop Detection — Why You Hear Yourself on Calls

Mic echo — sometimes called headset echo or room echo — occurs when your mic captures output that is already coming out of your output device. The captured signal is transmitted to the other person, played through their output unit, re-captured by their mic, and sent back to you, creating a loop. In a video call scenario, this sounds like a distinct delayed repeat of your own speech. In extreme cases, the loop amplifies itself into a high-pitched squeal — classic mic feedback that disrupts audience engagement and speech comprehensibility.

The echo correlation visualizer detects loop issues by measuring how strongly your current signal track correlates with a delayed copy of itself. A correlation coefficient approaching 1.0 at a lag greater than zero milliseconds is a strong indicator that a loop is present and active. The analysis panel flags this immediately so you can apply the fix before your next Zoom meeting or Google Meet session.

Worked Example 1: A remote worker joins a video call and immediately hears their own speech with a noticeable delay. They run the echo test: the echo correlation visualizer shows a sharp spike at approximately 80 ms lag and the detection flag turns red. The fix guide recommends switching from laptop output to earphones — with earphones, signal is delivered directly to the ears and cannot re-enter the mic, breaking the loop instantly.

Room Reverb and Spatial Reflection — Diagnosing Hard-Wall Environments

Even without a true loop, a bare room with hard walls, floors, and ceilings creates strong spatial reflection. Input bounces off these surfaces and arrives at your mic milliseconds after the direct signal. The result is a smeared, washy quality — sometimes called room reverb — that makes you sound like you are transmitting from a bathroom rather than a professional studio.

The reverb detection algorithm analyses the decay tail of each spoken word in your signal stream, measuring how long input lingers above the interference floor after you stop speaking. High reverberation times (above ~300 ms for a home office) are flagged in the analysis panel as a room acoustics warning. This is separate from mic latency — it is an environmental issue, not an equipment problem.

Worked Example 2: A live content creator captures in a bare concrete room. Viewers complain the output sounds muddy. Running this online tool reveals a high spatial reflection score and a reverb tail exceeding 400 ms. The fix: add soft furnishings (bookshelves, curtains, a sofa), use a noise-canceling headset with a directional capsule, or reposition the mic closer to the mouth to increase the direct-to-reverberant ratio. For content creation and professional work, acoustic panels provide the most reliable clarity improvement.

Why Your Microphone May Not Work — Troubleshooting Common Causes and Access Issues

If the waveform indicator stays flat after you speak, there are several possible causes. If you encounter a microphone not working scenario, most cases fall into one of these categories — use these steps to fix microphone issues and restore normal input:

  • Client permission denied: You clicked Block instead of Allow, or the mic permission expired. Reload the page, grant permission, and allow mic access again when prompted. Check your client settings to clear any blocked origins.
  • Mic blocked by another application: Zoom, a chat app, or Teams calls may have exclusive control of your signal input. Close Zoom and any other calling apps before retesting. You can also close other signal apps from Task Manager (Windows) or Activity Monitor (macOS).
  • Disabled mic in system settings: On Windows, open Sound Settings → Input and verify your mic is enabled. On macOS, check System Preferences → Security & Privacy → Microphone. On Linux, check PulseAudio or ALSA settings. A mic muted at the platform level prevents any client from accessing the signal source.
  • Driver issues / outdated drivers: Especially common with USB mic and USB headset units. Open Device Manager (Windows) and look for yellow warning icons under Audio Inputs. Download the latest driver from your manufacturer's website or run Windows Update. For a wireless or Bluetooth mic, ensure pairing is active and the unit appears in your platform's signal inputs.
  • Wrong input unit selected: If you have multiple mics — a built-in one, a webcam mic, and an external USB mic — the client may default to the wrong one. Use the selector dropdown to switch inputs and rerun the check.
  • Equipment failure: If testing on a second computer confirms the mic is also not detected there, the equipment may be faulty — a defective capsule or a damaged audio jack. Try a different USB port or cable, unplug and reconnect the unit, and test with a known-working headset mic to isolate the fault.
  • Mic stopped mid-test: This usually means another application took exclusive control of the signal stream mid-session. Close Zoom and chat apps, then reload the page.

Apply the Fix — Practical Steps to Eliminate Echo and Interference Suppression Issues

Once you know what it has found, use these ordered steps to resolve echo and loop issues and restore professional signal quality for your calls, content sessions, and captures:

  1. Switch to earphones or a noise-canceling headset: This is the single most effective fix for signal loops. Wired and wireless options both work — just ensure output goes to the earphones, not to external output units, so your mic cannot capture your own playback.
  2. Enable echo cancellation in your platform and app settings: In Windows Sound Settings, open your mic's properties and look for Acoustic Echo Cancellation or AEC enhancements. In macOS, check the signal preferences in your conferencing app. Many calling apps — Zoom, chat platforms, Google Meet — have dedicated echo cancellation toggles in their signal settings. Make sure it reads Yes in your mic details table.
  3. Reduce output volume: Lowering your output level reduces the amount of signal that can bleed into your mic. Even a 10–15 dB reduction can eliminate marginal loops.
  4. Reposition your mic: Move it away from output units and closer to your mouth. Correct positioning (6–12 inches, angled slightly off-axis) reduces room reverb pickup, ambient interference, and the risk of capturing your own playback. If it sounds muffled, you may be too close; if it sounds quiet or picks up ambient interference too readily, you may be too far — a classic problem for built-in laptop mics.
  5. Treat your room acoustics: Add soft materials — rugs, curtains, bookshelves filled with books — to reduce spatial reflection. For dedicated content creation or music capture spaces, acoustic foam panels mounted on walls deliver the best interference reduction without large investment.
  6. Update mic settings and drivers: If your mic details table shows automatic gain control as On and you are experiencing pumping interference, try disabling it in your platform's signal settings. For USB and integrated mics, check for firmware or driver updates.
  7. Use a noise-canceling headset mic or USB headset: Purpose-built mics with cardioid or super-cardioid polar patterns are specifically designed for clear interaction, rejecting input from the sides and rear while capturing your speech from directly in front. These are the best option for working from home, remote work, and conferencing scenarios.

Worked Example 3: A remote worker's mic details table shows echo cancellation as No. Their colleagues report a distracting echo on every Microsoft Teams call. The fix guide walks them through opening Windows Sound Settings → their mic's Properties → Enhancements tab → enabling Acoustic Echo Cancellation, then retesting with the online mic test to confirm the details table now reads Yes for echo cancellation. After applying the fix, the next test microphone run shows a clean correlation visualizer and a quality rating of Excellent.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is microphone echo?
Microphone echo occurs when your audio output (from speakers or headphones) is picked up by your microphone and re-transmitted, causing listeners to hear a delayed repeat of their own voice or yours. It's most common with open-back headphones, external speakers, or high microphone gain settings.
Why do I need a mic echo test?
Running an echo test helps you identify whether your microphone setup is causing echo, feedback loops, or clarity issues before important calls, recordings, or streams. Catching these problems early saves you from embarrassing audio quality during meetings or content creation.
How does this echo test work?
This tool simulates an echo diagnostic based on your microphone type, device, gain settings, echo delay, feedback level, and environment. It scores your configuration across key audio quality factors — echo level, feedback risk, clarity, and reverb tail — then gives you an overall rating and actionable recommendations.
How do I fix microphone echo?
The most effective fixes include enabling echo cancellation in your OS audio settings or communication app, reducing microphone gain, switching from open-back to closed-back headphones, using a directional (cardioid) microphone, and moving to a quieter, acoustically treated room. USB and XLR microphones typically have better built-in echo rejection than integrated mics.
Why doesn't my microphone work properly?
Common causes include browser permission being blocked (you must allow microphone access), another application monopolizing the mic (e.g., Zoom or Discord), incorrect input device selected in your OS settings, outdated drivers, or a hardware fault. Try closing other apps, checking privacy settings, and reconnecting your mic.
What is microphone feedback?
Feedback is the high-pitched squeal or howl that occurs when a microphone picks up its own amplified output in a loop. It's caused by speakers being too close to the mic or gain being set too high. Lowering gain, using headphones instead of speakers, and enabling noise suppression all help reduce feedback.
Does microphone type affect echo?
Yes. Integrated (built-in) microphones are most prone to echo because they're physically close to the device's speakers. USB and XLR microphones, especially directional ones, have better isolation and are significantly less susceptible to picking up speaker output. Wireless mics can introduce additional latency that increases perceived echo.
Is my audio uploaded or stored when I run this test?
No. This echo test tool runs entirely in your browser. No audio is recorded, uploaded, or stored on any server. All calculations are performed locally based on the settings you enter.
